Machine identities, tested in public · Chapter 2

The short version

The findings in plain words, and a map of every case.

  • 67 cases in four runs
  • 57 guesses matched
  • 10 guesses wrong
  • 20 cases with a control nothing noticed
  • 33 cases with a recorded limit

What I found

1. Most of my guesses held, which is weaker evidence than it sounds.

57 of 67 guesses matched. I wrote the guesses and the models, so a match shows that my reasoning is consistent. It does not show that a product behaves this way.

2. Ten guesses were wrong, and the wrong ones are the useful ones.

Five were about counting (S-R17-05, N-R14-07, N-R18-01, S-R17-09, P-R15-22). In two of those I never said what I was counting. Five were about which control does the work, or whether one covers another (N-R14-25, C-R16-C01, P-R15-06, P-R15-12, S-R17-08). Each is recorded with the right answer next to it.

3. In 20 cases a control sat there and nothing noticed when it was switched off.

In round 19 I split these into two kinds. Either another control was doing the same job, or the control had no effect on what the case counts, as with logs, reviews and thresholds. A control no check notices is one you cannot tell is working. Decide which it is before you rely on it or remove it.

4. A control that reports is not a control that stops.

In three chain tests an alert reported the attack after it happened, and a different control stopped it (C-R16-C05, C-R16-C06, C-R16-C17). Count the two separately.

5. Order decides whether a change causes an outage.

Update the consumer, then rotate, then prove the old secret is dead (N-R14-15, N-R14-20, P-R15-25). The shortcut caused 12 outages in one case and left 2 live old secrets in another.

6. A named owner did the most work.

Ownership stopped a chain at its first link (C-R16-C01) and turned silent risks into tickets (N-R14-09, N-R14-18). Everything else in the framework depends on knowing who answers for an identity.

7. Every case has a limit, and 33 of 67 have one written down.

Several controls accept a sign-off as text: an attestation, a break-glass reason, a ticket number. If the text is not checked against a system, the control checks that someone typed something.

What I would do on Monday

If you take only one page from the book, take this one. Each line is a check you can do without buying anything.

  1. Pick one source of service accounts you do not scan yet, and add it to the inventory (find).
  2. List identities with no named owner. Ask the platform owner to claim or stage them for disable (own).
  3. Look at your front door for new service accounts. Does it refuse one with no owner, scope or end date (own, limit)?
  4. Take the last rotation you did and test that the old secret is refused (rotate).
  5. Find the person who would see a break-glass sign-in tonight, and ask what they would do (watch).
  6. Write down which of your controls would go unnoticed if you switched it off (all steps).

How far to trust all of this

The guesses and the models are mine. The results show that my reasoning holds together. They do not show that a product behaves this way. Chapter 3 says what the method cannot tell you.
