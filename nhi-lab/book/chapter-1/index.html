Machine identities, tested in public · Chapter 1

The problem and what I tested

Why machine identities, what I asked, and what is in and out of scope.

Why machine identities

Ask a security team how many people have accounts and you will get a number. Ask how many service accounts, API keys, CI tokens, certificates and AI-tool grants they have, who owns each one and when each stops working, and the room goes quiet.

Palo Alto Networks says in a 2026 press release that machine identities outnumber human ones by more than 80 to 1 [S1]. That is a vendor statistic, and I could not find the study behind it. I use it as a reason to look, and I do not rely on the number.

A real case shows why it matters. Google's threat intelligence group reported that from 8 August 2025 an actor used stolen OAuth tokens tied to the Salesloft Drift app to read data in customers' Salesforce accounts. Salesloft and Salesforce revoked all active tokens on 20 August 2025 [S11]. Nobody had to guess a password. Someone held a token that still worked.

The question

What I set out to test

Do the controls people recommend for machine identities stop the failures they are meant to stop, and would anything tell you if one stopped working?

Why a lab, and why guesses first

I cannot test a vendor's product on a vendor's data. What I can test is the reasoning behind a control. If this rule exists, does this failure stop? And if I switch the rule off, does anything notice?

So each case is a small model of one control and one failure. I write down what I expect with the control off and on. I lock the file with a SHA-256 hash and commit it before any code exists. Then I write the model, run it, and compare. A test you design after seeing the result will agree with you. A committed guess file makes it visible if I changed my mind.
