THE DEFINITION

What "shadow AI" actually means

Not a hack. Not malware. AI running somewhere security didn't put it — and in most cases, didn't know to look for it.

Shadow AI is the use of AI tools, models, and agents outside an organization's security, procurement, and observability perimeter — a browser extension an employee installed personally, a copilot a SaaS vendor switched on by default, an MCP server an engineer connected without telling anyone. It's a direct descendant of shadow IT, the decades-old pattern of employees adopting unapproved software to move faster than procurement allows. What changed is the stakes: shadow IT was mostly data sitting somewhere ungoverned. Shadow AI is data in motion — pasted into prompts, routed to third-party model providers, and, in the case of agents, acted on autonomously, without a human approving each step.

The category didn't appear overnight — it rode the same curve that took "Shadow SaaS" from a niche IT-governance topic to a boardroom risk between 2023 and 2026, just faster. Within twenty days of ChatGPT going mainstream inside Samsung in 2023, the company had three separate confidential data leaks. By 2026, industry surveys put unsanctioned AI use anywhere from half to over 80% of the workforce — AI simply gave an existing shadow-IT surface a much sharper edge.

WHERE IT SPREADS

Knowingly, unknowingly — same blind spot

Shadow AI isn't one behavior. It's at least four different surfaces, and only one of them is a person deliberately going around IT.

01

Personal accounts and browser extensions — knowing

An employee signs up for ChatGPT, Claude, or a consumer AI browser extension with a personal or work email, entirely outside procurement. 78% of AI users at work admit to bringing their own unauthorized tools, and 88% have pasted work information into a public AI tool at least once.

02

AI features embedded in approved SaaS — unknowing

The productivity suite, CRM, or support platform an employee already uses ships a new AI copilot and switches it on by default. Nobody re-reviews it, because on paper it's the same vendor that was already approved — the AI feature inside never went through its own review.

03

Agentic browsers and MCP-connected agents — unknowing, then autonomous

Agentic browser assistants can click, fill forms, and submit data on a user's behalf, while agents that connect to tools through the Model Context Protocol talk agent-to-server rather than user-to-application — bypassing the SSO and CASB discovery built for human logins entirely. Roughly 80% of organizations now report unauthorized AI agent actions.

04

Browser extensions — the fastest-growing blind spot

A consumer AI browser extension installed under a corporate identity looks identical to any other tab — no separate binary, no admin approval, no CASB record. It's exactly the pattern behind the Vercel breach above: a single self-adopted extension, invisible to every inventory, became the entry point into a production environment and a downstream extortion campaign.

05

Network, cloud, and infra — the trail it leaves behind

Every surface above eventually touches the network: DNS lookups and proxy traffic to AI API endpoints, OAuth grants minted for AI apps, service accounts quietly calling model APIs, and unsanctioned AI deployments spun up directly inside cloud consoles. Invisible to a SaaS inventory — rarely invisible to the network, if anyone's looking.

THE RISK PROFILE

Why shadow AI breaks the old assumptions

Shadow AI doesn't look like a breach until well after it already is one.

01

Invisible by design

Shadow AI doesn't show up in asset inventories, CASB logs, or SSO reviews, because it's adopted outside procurement entirely — a browser extension, a personal account, a copilot switched on inside a SaaS tool nobody re-reviewed.

02

Most incidents aren't hacks

The typical shadow AI incident isn't an intrusion, it's normal use: pasting a customer record into a prompt box is enough. Surveyed employees admit to sharing research data (33%), employee records (27%) and financial data (23%) with tools nobody approved.

03

Leadership condones it

69% of C-suite respondents and 66% of directors and senior VPs say using unsanctioned AI tools is fine if it makes work faster — the governance gap starts at the top, not with a rogue employee.

04

Growing faster than the controls

Shadow-AI-linked breaches rose from 20% to 43% of AI-related incidents in a single year, and regular AI use on corporate devices tripled to 45% of employees in the 2026 Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report — up from just 15% the year before. The overwhelming majority of breached organizations had no AI governance policy and no adequate AI access controls in place at all.

THE DISCOVERY PLAYBOOK

What to analyze first, and how

Not a tool to buy. A sequence: four discovery mechanisms, run together, before a single policy gets drafted.

01

Network traffic analysis

DNS resolver logs, web proxy data, and TLS SNI inspection all capture the same signal: where traffic is actually going. A query matching destinations like api.openai.com, api.anthropic.com, and generativelanguage.googleapis.com against DNS logs catches the majority of shadow AI on its own.

02

API and inference monitoring

Network logs show where traffic goes; API monitoring shows what it's doing there — unauthorized model inference requests, unexpected token volumes, and calls that don't match any sanctioned integration.

03

Identity-based discovery

OAuth grants to unapproved AI apps, sign-ins to AI tools on personal rather than corporate accounts, and service accounts suddenly showing AI API activity — identity and SSO logs catch what network monitoring alone misses.

04

Cloud and SaaS enumeration

A direct scan of SaaS and IaaS environments for unsanctioned AI deployments, embedded copilots switched on by default, and MCP server connections that never appeared in an application inventory because they were never installed as an "application" at all.

None of these four alone is enough — each leaves its own blind spot. Correlated across DNS/proxy logs, CASB sign-up events, DLP paste and upload events, endpoint application inventory, and even expense reports, they produce an actionable map of an organization's real AI footprint in two to three weeks, not months.

AGENT GOVERNANCE

Do agents need their own policy? Yes — but inventory comes first

An AI agent isn't a user, and isn't quite an application either. Governing it means extending identity and policy programs to cover something that acts, not just something that logs in.

01

Inventory before policy

Agents connect to MCP servers agent-to-server, not user-to-application — bypassing discovery methods built around human identity entirely. The organizations governing agents effectively in 2026 are the ones that built an inventory of OAuth grants, API keys, and MCP connections first, not the ones that wrote the most comprehensive policy first.

02

Standards are arriving — unevenly

NIST's AI Agent Standards Initiative, the Berkeley CLTC Agentic AI Risk-Management Profile extending the NIST AI RMF, and Singapore's IMDA Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI all launched within weeks of each other in early 2026 — while the EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, and the original 2023 NIST AI RMF still contain no reference to "agent" or "agentic" at all.

03

Identity protocols exist; adoption doesn't, yet

MCP OAuth 2.1 under Linux Foundation governance, the Digital Identity Foundation's MCP-I and KYA-OS work, Microsoft Entra Agent ID, and the CSA Agentic Trust Framework already give agents a real identity layer to plug into, and NIST's NCCoE Concept Paper on Agent Identity and Authorization adds the missing piece: authentication, authorization, auditing, non-repudiation, and prompt-injection mitigation, specific to agents. Only 23.7% of organizations actually use their existing IAM or identity provider as the authorization server for their agentic infrastructure — most agents still run with no policy enforcement point at all.

04

Four controls, once visibility exists

Least privilege by default, scheduled access reviews, runtime monitoring of what each agent actually does with its permissions, and a real offboarding procedure for when an agent or its integration is retired — the same four controls identity teams already run for human accounts, extended to non-human ones.

05

What's coming next

NIST has an AI Agent Interoperability Profile scheduled for release in Q4 2026 — the first attempt at a shared standard for how agents from different vendors identify themselves to each other, not just to a human-run identity provider. Until it ships, every agent-to-agent handshake runs on vendor-specific glue.

A wave of purpose-built vendors emerged to discover and govern shadow AI — and in 2026, the biggest data-security platforms started buying their way in rather than building it themselves.

Visibility into AI use is being folded into core data-security platforms nearly as quickly as the shadow AI problem itself is growing.

WHAT'S AT STAKE

Data, compliance, revenue — in that order, fast

Shadow AI doesn't fail loudly. It fails quietly, for months, and then all three at once.

01

Data — gone, not just exposed

Once a prompt leaves the perimeter it can't be recalled, and some providers use submitted data for model training by default. Employees admit to sharing research data (33%), employee records (27%), and financial data (23%) with tools nobody approved — not through a breach, just through normal use.

02

Compliance — a filing, not just a fine

CB Financial Services' Form 8-K set the precedent: regulators now treat unauthorized AI use like a breach regardless of dollar loss. 68% of breached organizations had no AI governance policy in place at all when it happened — and the gap is widening, not closing: five of the six governance controls IBM tracks actually lost adoption year over year, and only 19% of breached organizations report security and governance teams working together at all.

03

Revenue — a measurable premium, then a projection

Shadow AI adds $670,000 to the average breach cost today, pushing shadow-AI breaches to $5.39M against a $4.99M baseline. Projected forward, unmanaged shadow AI is on track to cost enterprises more than $40 billion in remediation, compliance penalties, and lost productivity by 2027.

04

Trust — the second-order cost nobody prices in

After the Vercel breach, customer environment variables ended up fueling an extortion campaign on BreachForums — the cost wasn't just Vercel's, it was every downstream customer now explaining why their secrets were exposed. Shadow AI breaches don't stay contained to the company that caused them.

THE GUARDRAILS

What would have caught each incident

Not "write an AI policy." A discovery-first sequence applied before policy is even drafted.

01

Discovery before policy

You can't govern what you can't see. Continuous discovery of AI apps, agents, browser extensions and OAuth grants across the SaaS estate has to come before any policy is written, not after.

02

Sanctioned alternatives, not just bans

Samsung's response was an outright ban. Most organizations need a faster path: an approved tool that's as quick as the one people already reached for — or usage simply moves further into the shadows.

03

Data controls at the AI boundary

Classify and gate what data can reach any AI surface, sanctioned or not, since most incidents are prompt-level exposure rather than intrusions. DSPM-style controls matter more here than perimeter defenses.

04

Materiality-aware incident response

CB Financial shows regulators will treat AI misuse like a breach regardless of dollar loss. Incident response playbooks need an AI-use branch alongside the hacking branch, with its own disclosure criteria.
