AI Governance · Case study

You can’t govern the AI tools you don’t know exist

The AI a company approves and the AI its people use are two different lists. This is how to find the second one, work out what data it touches, respond without pushing it out of sight, and show the gap closing.

Independent research and control design · Last reviewed 1 Oct 2026

The short version

  • The list of approved AI tools is shorter than the list of tools in use. Security cannot protect what it has not found, so the first job is discovery, and it comes before the policy.
  • Discovery draws on several signals: network and proxy logs, browser and endpoint data, SaaS and OAuth grants, expense records, and the data stores themselves. Each finding is classified by the data it touches and gets one of four outcomes: allow, allow with controls, replace or block.
  • A block on its own moves the use somewhere you cannot see. The approved route has to be quicker than the one people found for themselves, and the gap is measured by how much use moves onto it.

The problem

Shadow AI is the use of AI tools, models and agents outside an organization’s security, procurement and observability perimeter. It might be a browser extension someone installed on their own, a copilot a SaaS vendor switched on by default, or a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server an engineer connected without telling anyone. It descends from shadow IT, where people adopted software faster than procurement could approve it.

The stakes are higher this time. Shadow IT was mostly data sitting somewhere ungoverned. With AI, data moves: it is pasted into prompts, sent to third-party model providers, and, when an agent is involved, acted on without a person approving each step. Much of this is ordinary use. Pasting a customer record into a prompt box is all it takes.

Nobody has to do anything wrong for the gap to open. Someone in finance wants a faster way to summarize contracts, a support lead finds a tool that writes up calls, an engineer wires an assistant into the repository. Each has a reasonable goal and no quick, approved way to meet it.

What has already happened

Three public cases show the range. I have kept to what the primary or closest sources say and noted where I could not reach a primary one.

  • Samsung, spring 2023. Bloomberg reported that Samsung restricted generative AI tools on company devices after staff put internal material into ChatGPT, including source code. Tech Monitor reported that the memo called the restriction temporary until safeguards were ready, and that the company was building its own tools. I read these through secondary coverage, not the Bloomberg article itself.
  • Vercel, April 2026. Vercel’s own bulletin says the incident began with a compromise of Context.ai, a third-party AI tool used by a Vercel employee. The attacker took over that employee’s Google Workspace account, reached the employee’s Vercel account and then internal systems, and could read environment variables that had not been marked sensitive. The bulletin does not say whether the tool was approved. Other write-ups describe an OAuth grant, and I treat that detail as secondary.
  • CB Financial Services, May 2026. The company filed an 8-K under Item 1.05 saying its bank subsidiary learned on 5 May 2026 of an internal incident in which non-public customer information was handled using an unauthorized AI-based software application. Customer names, Social Security numbers and dates of birth were involved, and the company said it did not expect a material impact on its financial results. I read the filing through a mirror site, not sec.gov.

The common thread is a tool the security team had not reviewed holding data or access it should not have had. Three stories say nothing about how common that is.

For scale, IBM’s Cost of a Data Breach Report 2025, based on 600 breached organizations in 17 industries, reports that 20% of the organizations studied had a breach linked to shadow AI, that shadow AI added up to USD 670K to the average breach cost, and that 63% of breached organizations lacked AI governance policies. That tells you what breaches looked like among organizations that had one. It does not tell you how many employees use unapproved AI. I have left out the usage surveys that circulate, because I could not trace their method and dates.

How discovery fits together

Five kinds of signal feed one inventory. Every entry in it gets a risk class based on the data it can reach, and a decision. The follow-up goes back to the person, and the next scan checks that the answer held.

Data security posture management (DSPM), described generally, inventories where data lives, labels what it is and shows what can reach it. In this picture it supplies the data half of the risk class. I make no claims for any particular product.

No single source is enough. Network logs show where traffic goes and say little about what it carried. Grant lists show what has access and miss tools that need none. Expense records catch paid tools and miss free ones. The value is in the overlap. A tool seen by three sources is almost certainly real, and one seen by a single source deserves a closer look.

From signal to decision, step by step

  1. Agree the rules of the sweep before you start

    Decide who may look at what, which logs are in scope, and what happens to the people you find. Tell staff that the aim is a complete list of the tools in use, and that the first answer to an unlisted tool is a conversation. Check monitoring rules with legal and HR for each country you operate in.

    GuardrailScope and monitoring basis approved in writingEvidenceSigned scope note and the message sent to staff

  2. Collect signals from every source you can reach

    Pull DNS and proxy records for known AI service names, the browser extension and installed-app lists from endpoint management, the app and grant lists from each SaaS admin console and the identity provider, card and expense lines, and scan repositories and shared storage for AI API keys. Each source misses something the others catch, so no single one counts as discovery.

    GuardrailAt least four independent source types per sweepEvidenceRun log showing each source, date and record count

  3. Turn each signal into one inventory record

    Many signals describe the same tool. Resolve them to one record with the tool name, the vendor, who uses it, the business owner, how it connects, and when it was first and last seen. A record with no owner stays open until someone is named.

    GuardrailNo record closes without a named business ownerEvidenceInventory export with owner and first and last seen dates

  4. Classify the data exposure

    Ask what data can reach the tool, on what kind of account, under what retention terms, and with what connected access. Give the record a class from 0 to 3 using the table below. A DSPM view of your data stores helps with the first question, because it shows where sensitive data sits and what can reach it.

    GuardrailClass assigned from the data, not from the tool's reputationEvidenceClassification record with the reasoning and the reviewer

  5. Choose one of four outcomes

    Allow, allow with controls, replace, or block. Each class has a default outcome, and the owner can argue for a different one. The decision is written down with its reason and a review date.

    GuardrailEvery record carries a decision and a review dateEvidenceDecision log entry

  6. Talk to the person before you enforce

    Ask what they were trying to get done. Most of the time the need is real and the approved tools do not cover it yet. Offer the approved route, or a short path to get the tool reviewed, and agree a date for the switch.

    GuardrailConversation comes before a block, except where regulated data is at stakeEvidenceCase note with the need, the offer and the agreed date

  7. Apply the controls at the boundary

    Turn the decision into settings: consent policy in the identity provider, extension allow lists, proxy categories, DLP rules, and a business account with the right retention terms. Keep the sanctioned route open on the same day you close the other one.

    GuardrailNo block without a working replacement offeredEvidenceChange records and configuration exports

  8. Re-scan and measure

    Run the sweep again on a schedule. A tool that comes back after a replace or block decision tells you the approved route is not good enough, so treat that as a finding about the route.

    GuardrailScheduled re-scan with results compared to the last runEvidenceTrend of the measures listed below

A simple way to classify exposure

This scheme is my own illustration. Adjust it to your data classes.

ClassWhat can reach the toolDefault outcome
0Public or no company data, such as marketing taglinesAllow and register it
1Internal material with no personal data, such as meeting notes about a projectAllow with controls: business account, retention terms, owner
2Confidential material, such as source code, contracts or unreleased numbersAllow only through an approved tool, otherwise replace
3Regulated or personal data, such as customer identifiers, health or payment dataBlock until an approved route and a review exist

Three modifiers move a record up or down a class. A personal account, where the company has no say over retention, moves it up. Terms that allow the provider to train on submitted data move it up. Connected access, such as an OAuth grant that can read all mail or an agent that can write to systems, can raise a class 1 tool to class 3, because the exposure then includes everything the grant can reach.

Example: a call-summary tool

This is an illustration, not a specific event.

What the sweep finds

  • A recurring card charge for a transcription subscription shows up in expense records.
  • The identity provider shows a calendar and mail grant to the same vendor from several accounts in one team.
  • Proxy logs show steady uploads to the vendor in the hours after customer calls.
  • Nobody is listed as owner.

What happens next

  • One inventory record joins the three signals and the team lead is named owner.
  • The calls contain customer personal data, so the record is class 3.
  • The decision is replace. The approved meeting platform has a summary feature that fits the need.
  • The grants are revoked on the day the team switches, and the next scan checks for the vendor.

The conversation with the team lead is the step that matters most. The need was real: customer calls had to be written up and the old way took too long. A block alone would have left the team with the same problem.

Responding without pushing it underground

If people learn that being found leads to punishment, they stop leaving traces. They move to personal phones and home networks, and the inventory gets worse exactly where the risk is highest. A few habits help.

  • Talk first. The first message about a found tool is a question about the job it does. Reserve formal action for repeated, knowing handling of regulated data in tools that were clearly off limits.
  • Block the exposure, not the category. If the problem is customer data going to a consumer tool, say that. People accept a rule about a risk more easily than a rule against a technology.
  • Open the replacement the same day. A block with no alternative is an instruction to find another tool. Samsung’s own response, as reported, included building in-house tools for the same reason.
  • Be open about what you can see. Tell people which signals you collect and why, within what legal and HR have agreed. Hidden monitoring that surfaces later damages trust far more than the findings were worth.
  • Treat repeat finds as feedback. When a tool returns after a replace decision, ask what the approved tool lacks.

How to roll it out without stalling

  1. 1. Agree the aim and the messageName an owner for the program, write the scope note, and tell staff what the sweep is for.Done whenLegal, HR, security and one business sponsor have signed the scope.
  2. 2. SeeRun the first sweep across the five signal sources and build the inventory, accepting that it will be incomplete.Done whenEvery record has at least one source, and the blind spots are written down.
  3. 3. Classify and decideApply the classes, walk each record with its owner, and log a decision with a review date.Done whenEvery record has an owner, a class and an outcome, or sits on the exception register with an end date.
  4. 4. Build the easy routeStand up the approved tools and a front door for requests, starting with the needs the sweep showed most often.Done whenA person can ask for a tool and get a clear answer in the time you promised.
  5. 5. Measure, then tightenRun scheduled sweeps, publish the measures, and close the old paths where the replacement works.Done whenNew unsanctioned tools are found quickly and mostly resolve through the front door.

Measuring the gap closing

Compare against your own baseline from the first sweep. I do not rely on outside benchmarks here, because their methods vary too much to compare with.

  • Share of records with an owner, a class and a decision. The core measure. The count of tools found will rise at first as discovery improves, so track the share with a decision, not the raw total.
  • Time from first sighting to decision. Measures how well the process works for the people waiting on it.
  • Traffic to approved endpoints against unapproved ones. Taken from the same proxy categories each month, so the trend means something. When it moves toward the approved route, the route is working.
  • Broad OAuth grants with no owner. Counts grants to AI apps that can read mail, files or calendars and have nobody accountable.
  • Repeat finds. Tools rediscovered after a replace or block decision.
  • Front door health. Requests received, median answer time, and the share approved, approved with controls, or declined.
  • Exceptions past their end date. A rising number means exceptions are being granted and forgotten.

Make the approved route the easy one

This is where most programs are won or lost. The shadow route takes minutes. The approved one has to compete.

  • Publish the approved list with what each tool is good for. People pick unapproved tools partly because they do not know what the approved ones can do.
  • Have a one-page request. Ask for the job to be done, the data involved and the team. Anything more and people skip it.
  • Promise an answer time and keep it. A simple answer in days beats a perfect one in months. A fast lane for class 0 and 1 requests keeps simple asks moving.
  • Review once and reuse. When one team’s request is approved, every team gets the result, with the same controls.
  • Provision accounts for approved tools. If the business account takes a ticket and the personal one takes a minute, you will lose.
  • Review AI features as well as vendors. A new copilot inside an approved product gets its own look.

Governance of agents follows the same logic and adds identity. An agent that connects to tools needs an owner, scoped access, a record of what it did and a way to switch it off, the same as a service account does. My working rules follow the same idea for my own use of AI: AI advises, people decide.

What tends to go wrong

  • The first inventory is bigger than anyone expected. Agree beforehand that it is a starting point and not a verdict on the teams.
  • A ban is announced before the replacement exists. Use goes quiet, then resurfaces on personal devices where you cannot see it.
  • One source is treated as the whole picture. Network logs alone miss features inside approved apps. A grant list alone misses tools that need no grant.
  • Classes are set by brand. A well-known tool on a personal account can be a bigger exposure than a lesser-known one on a business account with fixed retention terms.
  • The front door is slow. If the answer takes weeks, people stop asking.
  • Monitoring surprises people. Staff who find out later that their traffic was being reviewed stop trusting the program.
  • Exceptions pile up. Without end dates, the register becomes the new shadow list.

What I have learned to do differently

These are habits from how I approach this work. They do not describe any one project.

  • I ask about the job before the tool. The first question to someone using an unlisted tool is what they were trying to get done. The answer usually tells me what to build.
  • I start with the pain, as I do in a privileged access program. I talk to the business units first, then design. The same order applies here: what slows people down, what audit keeps asking about, which teams are under pressure.
  • I walk the inventory through with each owner. A list nobody has agreed to is a list nobody acts on. I want each owner to recognize their records and sign off the decision.
  • I write the rules down and get them approved. Classes, outcomes, exception criteria and review dates are documented, so they survive when people change roles.
  • I let tools sort and people decide. Software can group signals and suggest a class. A person confirms it, because the context lives with the owner. AI and people work together here, with security, governance, monitoring and collaboration around them.
  • I treat a repeat find as a design bug. If a tool keeps returning, I look at what the approved route is missing before looking at the person.
