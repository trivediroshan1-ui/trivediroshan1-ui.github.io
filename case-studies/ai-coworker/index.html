AI as a Security Product Co-worker

How to bring an AI assistant into a working team: what it may read, do and decide, what it can reach, and who answers for it.

Practitioner and workflow designer · Last reviewed 1 Oct 2026

The short version

  • AI is the future of this work, and people cannot be replaced in it. The two work together, with security, governance, monitoring and collaboration around them.
  • Treat the assistant like a new team member. It gets a job, a named owner, its own identity, access limited to that job, and a written agreement about what it does alone and what needs a yes.
  • Every connection to a company tool is an access decision. Put a broker and a policy in front of it, keep a human checkpoint on anything that writes, sends or leaves the company, and log all of it.

The problem

AI can speed up product and security work. I use OpenAI, ChatGPT and Claude to structure research, compare options and tighten executive writing. The speed is real. So are the risks of ungoverned use: data leaves the building, a confident answer turns out to be wrong, and nobody can say afterwards who decided what.

That is the early picture, when an assistant is a browser tab and a person pastes text into it. It changes quickly once the assistant is connected to things. An assistant that can read the shared drive, query the ticket system and send a message is no longer a tool you consult. It acts, and it acts with some set of permissions. Those permissions belong to somebody. If nobody decided what they should be, they were decided by whoever set up the connection that afternoon.

The Model Context Protocol, MCP, makes the connection easy to build. That is useful, and it also means a new kind of non-human identity can appear in a team in a day. The questions I would ask of any new joiner apply here too. What is your job? Who do you report to? What can you see? What can you change? Who checks your work? Most teams have clear answers for people and no answers for the assistant sitting next to them.

This page describes how I would answer those questions. It is a reference approach for a team that wants the speed without giving up accountability.

The idea: a coworker with a job description

A coworker has a role. They have a manager, a login, a scope of work and a list of things they should ask about first. When they leave, their access is removed the same week. I apply all of that to an AI assistant, on purpose and in those words, because the habits people already have for new colleagues are good habits.

Three questions sit under everything else.

  • What may it read? Which sources, at which classification, for which task.
  • What may it do? Which actions, in which systems, with what limit on size and reach.
  • What may it decide? Almost nothing that matters. It can recommend, draft and challenge. A person decides, and the person is named.

The answers go into a short working agreement, one page, written before the assistant is connected to anything. The agreement is the thing the rest of this page hangs on.

A working agreement, in plain terms

Kind of workOn its ownWith approvalNever
ReadingSources the owner has listed for the task, at public or internal levelA new source, or a source with more sensitive contentAnything above the level the agreement names, even if the connection technically allows it
WritingDrafts, summaries and notes saved where only the owner can see themAnything another person will read as coming from the teamPublishing, or sending outside the company
Changing systemsNothing at firstLow-risk, reversible changes, one at a time, after the pilotChanging access rights, credentials or its own permissions
DecidingWhich of two drafts to show firstEverything that affects a person, a customer or a controlApproving its own work, or another assistant’s

The columns are deliberately plain. If a working agreement needs a lawyer to read it, nobody will follow it. The owner signs it, the team lead sees it, and it is reviewed whenever the assistant gets a new connection.

Data: what it may read and what can leave

Data classification is the part teams already have and rarely apply to AI. The company has levels, usually something like public, internal, confidential and restricted. The working agreement states which levels the assistant may read for each task, and which of those may leave the company boundary, for example by going to an external model service.

I keep two separate questions apart because they get blurred. The first is whether the assistant may read a source. The second is where that content goes once it is read. A source can be fine to read inside the company and still not be fine to send to a service outside it. The agreement answers both.

LevelAssistant may readMay leave the companyExample of how I handle it
PublicYesYesPublished standards, vendor documentation, my own published pages
InternalYes, for listed tasksOnly to services the company has approved for that levelMeeting notes, process documents, draft plans
ConfidentialOnly with owner approval per taskNoCustomer details, contracts, architecture diagrams
RestrictedNoNoCredentials, keys, personal data, audit findings under review

My own rule for this site is stricter than most companies need. Nothing confidential goes in. No employer, client or internal operational data, which is why every case study here is anonymized. In a company the line moves, but the habit stays: check the level before the paste.

OWASP lists sensitive information disclosure among the top risks for applications built on language models, and it names credentials, personal data and confidential business data as the kinds of content at risk. It also warns that restrictions given to a model may not always be honored, and could be bypassed by prompt injection. That is why I do not rely on telling the assistant to keep a secret. The control is that the secret is never in reach.

How it fits together

The diagram shows one request. A person asks their coworker for something. The coworker has its own identity and does not borrow the person’s login. When it needs a company tool, whether that is reached through MCP or a plain API, the call goes through an access broker. The broker checks policy and hands back narrow, short-lived access for that call. Tools are grouped by what they can do: read, draft or act. Anything high impact stops at a human checkpoint, and the answer goes back to the person it acts for. An audit trail records every step.

The MCP specification is useful here because it puts the access question in the protocol. Authorization is optional in MCP. When it is used over HTTP, the specification builds on OAuth 2.1 and treats the MCP server as a resource server. It requires clients to say which server a token is for, and it requires servers to accept only tokens issued for them. It also forbids a server from passing the token it received on to another API. If the server calls an upstream service, it uses a separate token for that. For a local server over standard input and output, the specification says to read credentials from the environment instead. I take two things from this. The assistant should hold narrow tokens that work in one place only, and a tool server in the middle should never become a way to launder access.

The specification also has the server tell the client which scope an operation needs, so the client asks for no more and steps up only when an operation requires it. That maps well to the checkpoint: the first request carries read scope, and a write needs a fresh decision.

Bringing the assistant on, step by step

  1. Name the job and the owner

    Write what the assistant is for in one or two sentences. Name one person as its owner, and name the person it acts for if that is someone else. The owner answers for what it does. If nobody will put their name on it, it does not start.

    GuardrailNo named owner, no assistantEvidenceSigned working agreement

  2. Classify what it will touch

    List the sources it needs and the level of each. Decide which can be read, which can leave the company, and which are out. Remove from the plan any source the job does not need.

    GuardrailSources listed per task, nothing impliedEvidenceSource and classification list

  3. Give it its own identity

    The assistant gets a distinct account or token, tagged to its owner. It does not use the owner’s login. That keeps its actions separate from the person’s in every log, and it means its access can be changed or removed without touching the person’s.

    GuardrailSeparate identity, no shared or borrowed credentialsEvidenceIdentity record with owner field

  4. Connect tools through the broker

    Each tool is added by request, with the narrowest scope that does the job. Read access is the default. Write access is a separate request with its own reason. Tokens are short-lived and issued for the specific server.

    GuardrailLeast privilege, scoped and short-lived tokensEvidencePolicy as configured, token lifetime

  5. Mark the checkpoints

    Decide which actions wait for a person: anything that writes to a system of record, sends a message, changes access, or involves content above the agreed level. The checkpoint shows the person what the assistant intends to do, in plain words, and what it relied on.

    GuardrailHuman approval for high-impact actionsEvidenceApproval record with approver and time

  6. Pilot read-only, then widen

    Start with reading and drafting. Review the output and the logs on a fixed rhythm. Add one capability at a time, and only after the previous one has been reviewed.

    GuardrailOne new capability per reviewEvidenceReview notes, change log of permissions

  7. Log and watch

    Decide which events are recorded: requests, tool calls, data sources read, outputs, approvals, refusals and errors. Send them to the place where the security team already looks. Set alerts for the odd cases, such as a new tool, a denied call or unusual volume.

    GuardrailLogging on before the first real taskEvidenceLog sample and alert rules

  8. Review, then offboard when the job ends

    On a schedule, the owner confirms the assistant is still needed and the access is still right. When the job ends, or the owner moves on, the identity is disabled, tokens are revoked, connections are removed and the working agreement is closed. An unowned assistant is a standing account with nobody watching it.

    GuardrailRegular owner attestation, access removed on exitEvidenceReview record, revocation record

Example: preparing a quarterly access review (illustrative)

This is an illustration of the model, not a real case. Imagine a team lead who owns a quarterly review of who has access to a set of internal applications. She wants a coworker to do the preparation, and she is both the owner of the assistant and the person it acts for.

  • Agreement. The assistant may read an export of the access list, classified internal, and the team’s review procedure. It may write a draft summary into the lead’s own workspace. It may not send anything, and it may not change access.
  • Request. The lead asks for a summary of accounts that look unusual against the procedure: no recent use, no manager recorded, or broad access compared with the rest of the group.
  • Work. The assistant reads the export and the procedure, using read-only access through the broker. It produces a list with, for each item, which rule it checked and which rows it relied on.
  • Checkpoint. The assistant proposes reminder messages to the application owners. Messages go to other people, so the lead sees the draft and the recipient list and approves, edits or drops each one.
  • Decision. The lead decides what to do about each flagged account. The assistant has no say, and it is not asked to.
  • Evidence. The log shows the export it read, the draft it wrote, the messages proposed, which the lead approved and when. An auditor can follow the whole thing without asking anyone.

The result is that the lead spends her time on judgment and the assistant spends its time on the reading. If the assistant misreads a row, the evidence column makes that visible in minutes, and nothing has been sent or changed in the meantime.

Rolling it out in phases

I would not turn this on for everyone at once. A staged rollout is slower for a month and faster after that, because each stage proves a control before the next one relies on it.

  1. Phase 0. Write it downWorking agreement template, classification levels, naming for assistant identities, and the owner role. Nothing is connected yet.Exit checkTwo or three people can explain the agreement back in their own words.
  2. Phase 1. Read and draftOne small team, read-only sources at public and internal level, drafts saved privately. Logs are on and someone reads them.Exit checkThe team can answer the audit questions below from the logs alone.
  3. Phase 2. Propose and approveThe assistant proposes actions and messages. A person approves each one. Checkpoints are tuned so they are neither skipped nor constant.Exit checkApprovers say the checkpoint shows enough to decide quickly.
  4. Phase 3. Act within limitsLow-risk, reversible actions in named systems, still through the broker. Anything outside the list stays at a checkpoint.Exit checkEach action type has an owner, a rollback and a log entry.
  5. Phase 4. Run it like a teamRegular access review for assistant identities, offboarding tested on a real exit, and the agreement reviewed when tools or models change.Exit checkA removed assistant leaves no live access behind.

Guardrails

Each guardrail is tied to a risk it answers. OWASP names the risk of excessive agency as damaging actions taken in response to unexpected, ambiguous or manipulated model output, and it traces it to too much functionality, too many permissions or too much autonomy. Those three are a useful checklist for any connection I review.

GuardrailWhat it preventsHow I check it
Narrow tool set per jobExcessive functionality: a tool that can do more than the job needsCompare each tool’s capabilities to the working agreement
Read by default, write by requestExcessive permissions: a read task holding write accessList granted scopes per connection
Human approval for high-impact actionsExcessive autonomy: irreversible actions taken on a bad outputTry a high-impact action in the pilot and confirm it stops
Authorization in the downstream systemRelying on the model to decide what it is allowed to doTry a forbidden call directly and confirm the tool refuses it
Own identity, short-lived tokensBorrowed logins, long-lived secrets in a chat or a configCheck token lifetime and the owner field on the identity
Untrusted content kept apartPrompt injection: instructions hidden in a web page or file the assistant readsFeed a test document that contains an instruction and see what the assistant does with it
Classification limits on reading and sendingSensitive information leaving the companySample outputs and outbound calls against the level list

On prompt injection, OWASP separates direct injection, where the user’s own input changes the model’s behavior, from indirect injection, where the model reads outside content such as a website or a file that carries instructions. The second kind matters most for a coworker, because reading outside content is its job. OWASP’s mitigations match what I already do for people: restrict privileges to the minimum, put a human in the loop for privileged operations, and keep untrusted content clearly separated. A hijacked prompt can only do what the assistant could already do, which is the argument for narrow access.

What an auditor will ask

I design the logging by starting from the questions that will be asked later. If the answer cannot be found from the record, the logging is incomplete.

QuestionWhere the answer comes from
Who owns this assistant, and who is it acting for?The identity record and the signed working agreement
What was it allowed to read and do on that date?The change history of its scopes and connections
What did it actually read and write?Tool-call logs from the broker and the tools themselves
Which actions did a person approve, and who?Approval records with approver, time and the proposed action
Did any sensitive data leave the company?Outbound call logs checked against the classification rules
What happens when it is wrong?Incident procedure, rollback steps, and past review notes
How is it removed?The offboarding checklist and the revocation record

NIST SP 800-53 gives the logging a structure I find useful. AU-2 asks the organization to decide which event types are logged, to explain why those events support investigations, and to review the list on a schedule. AU-12 asks that the system can generate the audit records for those events, and that authorized staff can choose which events are logged. For an assistant, I add a few event types to the usual list: a tool call, a data source read, an approval requested, an approval given or refused, and a permission change.

Governance: who owns what

NIST AI RMF is clear that accountability structures should exist so that the right teams and individuals are empowered, responsible and trained, and it calls for documented roles and responsibilities for human and AI configurations and for oversight. I turn that into a short list of roles for the team.

RoleOwns
Assistant ownerThe job, the working agreement, the access requests and the periodic review. Answers for what it does.
The person it acts forThe approvals at the checkpoints. Owns the decisions the assistant only recommends.
Identity and access teamIdentity creation, the broker and policy, token lifetimes and removal on exit.
Security and data ownersClassification levels, what may leave the company, monitoring rules and incident response.
Internal auditIndependent checking, separate from those who run the controls.
  • Accountability stays with a person. The assistant can draft and challenge. I read it, change it and own what gets published or recommended. The same sentence works for any team member.
  • Review the agreement when something changes. A new tool, a new model or a new data source is a reason to reopen it. Quiet changes are where drift starts.
  • Exceptions have owners and end dates. If a team needs a wider scope for a month, that is written down, approved by the data owner and removed when it ends.
  • Measure what you can defend. Useful measures: assistants with a named owner, assistants past their review date, and denied calls. I set no targets before I have a baseline.

What tends to go wrong

  • The connection arrives before the agreement. Somebody connects a tool because it takes ten minutes, and the working agreement is written later, if at all. The access then reflects convenience.
  • The assistant borrows a person’s login. Every action looks like the person did it. Logs stop being useful, and the person ends up answering for something they did not do.
  • Checkpoints turn into noise. If every action asks for approval, people click yes without reading. If nothing asks, nothing is checked. The fix is fewer checkpoints on the actions that matter, with enough detail to decide quickly.
  • Reading is treated as harmless. A read-only connection can still carry a confidential document into a service outside the company, or pull in a page with hidden instructions. Reading needs classification and content handling too.
  • The assistant is never offboarded. The pilot ends, the owner changes team, and the identity and its tokens stay. This is the same orphaned-account problem as with service accounts, and it needs the same ownership discipline.
  • Fear makes people hide it. If the rules are too strict to work with, people use personal accounts and tabs instead, and the company sees none of it. A usable approved path is part of the control.

What I have learned to do differently

These are habits from how I work and how I run programs. None of them comes from a single incident.

  • I write the agreement first. Before I connect anything, I write down what the assistant is for, what it may read and what needs my yes. It takes a short time, and it settles arguments later.
  • I check sources myself. If a claim matters, I trace it to something I can link to. A confident answer is not a source, and I treat my own use of AI the same way I would ask a team to treat theirs.
  • I decide the data level before I paste. Nothing confidential goes in. If I am not sure about a document, I treat it as more sensitive until I find out.
  • I start read-only. A new capability is a new decision. I add one at a time and look at the logs between each.
  • I keep the decision with a person. AI advises and people decide. I use it to find gaps in my reasoning and to tighten writing, and I make the call.
  • I design the audit answer before the feature. I ask what a reviewer will want to know in a year, and I check the record contains it.
  • I plan the exit at the start. Who removes the access, how, and how I know it worked. It is much easier to write on day one than to reconstruct later.
  • I apply it to myself first. If I cannot run my own workflow with scoped access, clear ownership, evidence and a person who can say no, I have no business recommending it to anyone else.

The same thinking runs through my page on how I use AI, and where I stop, and through the way I treat machine accounts in Non-Human Identity at Scale. An assistant with credentials is another non-human identity, and it benefits from the same ownership rules.
