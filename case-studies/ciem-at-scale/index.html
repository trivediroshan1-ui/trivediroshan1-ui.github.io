Cloud Entitlement Management (CIEM) at Scale

How to find the toxic entitlement combinations that individual cloud IAM rules miss, cut excess access from what people and workloads actually use, and keep it from growing back.

Credential Management Platform Owner & PAM Product Owner · Last reviewed 1 Oct 2026

The short version

  • Cloud identities hold far more access than they use. That gap is the main thing a cloud entitlement program works on, and each cloud already records the usage data needed to measure it.
  • Reviewing one role or one policy at a time misses combinations. What an identity can really do is the union of everything attached to it, plus whatever it can reach through trust links to other accounts.
  • Every cut goes to an owner for approval, ships with a rollback, and is backed by policy as code and organization-level guardrails, so the excess does not return.

The problem

Cloud permissions pile up. A role is created for a project and given broad rights so the launch does not slip. A person changes teams and keeps their old access. A pipeline’s service account gets one more policy each time a deploy fails. Nobody takes anything away, because removing access can break something and nobody can say for sure that it will not.

Four kinds of identity sit in that pile: people, workloads, federated identities that arrive through a corporate directory or an outside provider, and service accounts that exist for one job. Each cloud models them in its own way. The names, the policy languages and the ways of attaching permissions all differ, so a view built for one cloud tells you little about the next.

Two problems come out of this. The first is size. Granted permissions are much larger than used permissions, and that gap is attack surface. The second is shape. When policies are reviewed one at a time, combinations go unseen. An identity can hold two permissions that each look reasonable and that together let it change its own access, or reach data that another account controls. The real entitlement is the union of every policy attached to the identity. In the program this page describes, that was the starting point: policies reviewed singly, with risky combinations found late or by chance.

Federated identities deserve a second look. The trust relationship that lets an outside identity assume a role is an entitlement in its own right. Reading only the permissions on the role misses who is allowed to become it.

Granted, effective and used

Three words carry most of the confusion in this area, so I define them early and keep them fixed.

  • Granted is what is written in the policies attached to an identity.
  • Effective is what the identity can do once everything is combined: direct policies, group or role membership, resource policies, permission boundaries, organization-level guardrails, deny rules and trust relationships. Effective permissions are what an attacker inherits.
  • Used is what the logs show the identity did over a stated window.

The work is the gap between effective and used, and the paths hidden inside effective. The three major clouds each publish usage data that helps. The table below summarizes what their documentation says, without ranking them.

Two limits from the AWS documentation shape how I use any of this. Policy generation leaves out data events and the iam:PassRole action, which CloudTrail does not record, and last accessed data excludes data plane events. A permission can look unused because the log cannot see it. Absence from the log is evidence, and a person still decides.

One product note, since it comes up. Microsoft announced end of sale for Microsoft Entra Permissions Management from 1 April 2025, with retirement later that year. Its own pages give slightly different retirement dates, 1 October and 1 November 2025, so I do not count on it and I check the provider’s current documentation before building around any product. For Azure, I use Entra access reviews and Privileged Identity Management for review and removal, plus my own analysis for effective permissions.

How it fits together

Collection is read-only. Each account, subscription or project feeds three kinds of data into one analysis step: which identities exist, what permissions they hold, and what they did. The analysis has two parts. Rules compute effective permissions and the gap against use. A second, model-assisted pass reads the same graph and proposes combinations worth a look, for example an identity that can create a role and also attach policies to it. That pass only advises. It cites the policy statements behind every finding, and it has no path to change a policy.

One finding, start to finish

The same eight steps apply to a human, a workload, a federated role or a service account. Each has a guardrail and leaves evidence.

  1. Inventory the identities and name an owner

    List every identity in every account, subscription and project, tagged by kind. Each gets a named owner. An identity with no owner is the first thing to review, because nobody else can speak for it.

    GuardrailNo owner, no standing accessEvidenceInventory record with owner

  2. Compute effective permissions

    For each identity, combine every source: direct and inherited policies, resource policies, boundaries, guardrails, deny rules and trust relationships, including links to other accounts. This is the picture that single-policy review never produces.

    GuardrailCollection runs with read-only accessEvidenceDated effective-permission snapshot

  3. Join it with observed use

    Pick a window that covers the longest regular job, then compare. AWS unused access analysis, for one, only judges permissions that have existed for the whole tracking period, and I apply the same rule everywhere so new identities are not judged early.

    GuardrailWindow at least as long as the longest regular jobEvidenceUsage report with window dates

  4. Look for escalation paths and toxic combinations

    Rules flag the known patterns. The advisory pass looks for new ones, such as permission sets that let an identity widen its own access or reach another account’s data. Each finding lists the policy statements it rests on, so a reviewer can check it without trusting the story.

    GuardrailA finding that cites no statements is discardedEvidenceFinding record with cited statements

  5. Draft the smallest change that keeps real work running

    Remove what is unused first. Narrow wildcards to observed resources. Split roles that serve two jobs. Add by hand any permission the logs cannot show. Run the draft through policy validation before a person sees it.

    GuardrailPolicy checks pass before reviewEvidenceBefore and after diff

  6. Approve

    The resource owner confirms the work still runs. Security confirms the risk. This is the same approval gate used for standing privileged access changes, so remediation follows a process people already know and auditors already trust.

    GuardrailThe author of a change cannot approve itEvidenceApproval record

  7. Apply in stages, with a rollback ready

    Start in the lowest-risk environment. Keep the previous policy in version control and rehearse the restore before the cut. Watch denied calls for a set period afterwards and treat a new denial as a signal to look, not as proof the cut was wrong.

    GuardrailRollback tested before the changeEvidenceChange record and rollback copy

  8. Keep it from growing back

    Move the new policy into code, so the repository is the source of truth. Put organization-level boundaries in place for the grants nobody should make. Re-run the loop on a schedule.

    GuardrailConsole changes outside the pipeline raise an alertEvidenceDrift report and review record

Example: a nightly pipeline role

This is an illustration, not a specific event.

Before

  • Two broad policies give write access to every storage bucket and every queue in the account.
  • The role can also create roles and attach policies to them, which is a path to more access.
  • A trust link lets a second account assume it, left over from a migration that finished long ago.
  • The owner field is empty.

After

  • Write access covers one bucket prefix and one queue, which is what the logs showed.
  • The ability to create roles and attach policies is gone.
  • The trust link is removed after the owning team confirmed the migration was over.
  • A named team owns the role, and the change sits in version control with its rollback.

Three checks sat between the logs and the cut. The first window was 90 days, and the team mentioned a quarterly job, so we widened the window and held that permission until the owner confirmed. The permission to pass a role to another service does not appear in the usage data, so we reviewed it by hand. And the rollback copy was restored once in a test account before the change went to production.

Keeping it from growing back

A cleanup that ends is a cleanup that reverses. Three mechanisms work together.

  • Policy as code. Policies live in a repository and ship through a pipeline. AWS describes custom policy checks in IAM Access Analyzer that compare an updated policy with the existing one and report new access, or confirm that specified access is not allowed. Wired into the pipeline, a change that adds access stops there for review.
  • Organization-level boundaries. Service control policies in AWS Organizations do not grant permissions. They set the outer limit of what can be granted, so an undesired permission cannot take effect even when an attached policy allows it. They govern principals inside the organization, and they do not apply to the management account. Google Cloud offers deny policies, which override allow policies. Both are blunt tools, so I write each one narrowly, with a named exception route.
  • A rhythm of review. Entra access reviews, with owners or managers as reviewers, give a scheduled way to confirm human and service principal access. For the rest, I rerun the analysis on a calendar and compare it with the last snapshot.

  • Exceptions have owners and end dates. Anything left broad is on a register, reviewed on a rhythm, and removed when its reason ends.
  • Measure what you can defend. Useful measures: identities with no owner, trust links with no reason, open exceptions past their end date, and time from finding to approved change.
  • Use the gate people already know. Access changes to cloud identities follow the same approval path as standing privileged access, so there is one process to audit.

What tends to go wrong

  • The logs have blind spots. Some actions are not recorded at action level, and some cannot be seen in usage data at all. A permission can look unused and still be needed.
  • The window is too short. Quarterly and yearly jobs show up in nobody’s 90-day report, and they fail at the worst moment.
  • Cuts land too fast. One broken service teaches a whole company to resist the next cleanup. Go in stages, and start with what has no owner and no use.
  • Tool output gets treated as the answer. A report of unused access is a question for the owner. It is not a decision.
  • Trust links are missed. Each account looks fine on its own, and the problem sits in the link between two of them.
  • Blunt guardrails cause outages. An organization-level deny or boundary applies everywhere it is attached. A wrong one blocks legitimate work across many teams, so test it first in a small scope.
  • Fixes drift back. Without policy as code and alerts on console changes, the next urgent deploy puts the wildcard back.

What I have learned to do differently

These are habits I keep, not a record of any single project.

  • I start with identities nobody owns. That is the smallest argument to win, and it builds the approval routine on easy cases before hard ones.
  • I ask for the job calendar before I pick a window. One conversation with the service team about what runs yearly saves a lot of rollbacks.
  • I write the change in plain words next to the diff. Owners confirm what they understand. A raw policy diff gets a quick yes that means little.
  • I treat the report as a question. I take it to the owner with the evidence and let them tell me what it misses.
  • I rehearse the rollback. Restoring a policy in a test account takes minutes and changes how calm everyone is on the day.
  • I keep the model in an advisory role and make it show its work. A finding that cites the statements behind it can be checked in a minute. One that does not gets dropped.
  • I write the exception route into the guardrail. People will find a way around a boundary with no door. A documented door with an owner and an end date is easier to watch.
  • I report open exceptions, not a single score. A number invites debate about the number. A list of named exceptions gets closed.
