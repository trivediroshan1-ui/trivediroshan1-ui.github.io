Privileged Access Migration Planning

How I plan a move from one privileged access tool to another, so that nothing the old tool quietly did gets lost on the way.

Analysis and migration lead · Last reviewed 1 Oct 2026

The short version

  • A migration moves more than accounts. The old tool also carries controls, approvals, integrations and habits, and some of them were never written down.
  • I start by analysing what the current tool does, what it fails to do and what is inside it. Then I run discovery and get the plan signed off before anything moves.
  • Waves follow dependency, risk, access criticality and rollback readiness. At cutover every secret is rotated, because whatever sat in the old vault has to be treated as known to the people who administered it.

The problem

A privileged access migration has to preserve controls, user journeys, integrations and operational continuity. Moving the accounts is the easy part. The hard part is everything around them: the approval that fires before a checkout, the nightly script that reads a password from the old tool’s API, the alert someone relies on, the administrator who knows which safe to open at 2 a.m.

The old tool has usually been running for years. People built on it without telling anyone. If you only copy the inventory you will move the accounts and lose the behaviour. Most of my planning work is about finding that behaviour early and deciding, on paper, what happens to each piece of it.

I led the analysis of the ERPM PAM tool and the capability mapping for the target-state decision. The method below is what that work taught me to do every time.

Start with the tool you already have

When an organisation already has a tool and wants to move to a new one, I do not start with the new one. I start with three questions about the old one.

  • What does it do today? Features, integrations, and how people really use it, which is often different from how it was designed.
  • What is it failing to do? The gaps that made the organisation want to move. These become requirements for the target, so they are worth writing down in the owners’ own words.
  • What is inside it? The inventory of accounts, safes or folders, policies and owners. I treat this as a claim to be checked, not as a fact.

The answers feed a capability map: one row per thing the old tool does, and what the target does about it. That map is what the target-state decision and the later control-equivalence checks are built on.

What is in the inventory

I split the inventory into five classes, because each one migrates differently and each one has a different person to ask.

ClassExamplesThe question I askWhat makes it tricky
Human privilegedDomain, local and root admin accounts, database administratorsWho actually uses it, and how often?People have habits and saved sessions. Workflow and approval rules have to match or people work around them.
Service and applicationService accounts, application and database logins, API keysWhat reads this, and what breaks if it changes?Changing the value can stop a job. This is where hidden dependencies live.
SharedAccounts used by a team, vendor or help deskWho is in the group, and who is it really?Without individual accountability, the session record is the only evidence of who did what.
MachineSSH keys, certificates, tokens, secrets used by pipelines and workloadsCould this be short-lived instead of stored?Some stores hold them in fields nobody tracks. Some cannot accept a rotated value.
Break glassEmergency accounts for when sign-in or the platform is downDoes it work if everything else is broken?It must follow organisation policy and be tested, because it is the way back in during a failed cutover.

Discovery: finding what nobody wrote down

The exported inventory tells you what the tool believes. Discovery tells you what is true. I use several sources and compare them, because each one is wrong in a different way.

SourceWhat it showsHow it misleads
The old tool’s own account listWhat was onboarded, with owners and policiesOwners have left, and accounts were added and never removed.
Usage and audit logs of the old toolWho checked out what, when it last happened, which API clients called itQuiet accounts may still matter once a quarter or once a year.
Scans of servers, directory and network devicesPrivileged accounts that exist but were never vaultedA scan finds accounts, not the reason they exist.
Scheduled tasks, scripts and pipeline codeJobs that call the old tool or hard-code a credentialCode search misses what lives in someone’s head or on a laptop.
Tickets and approval historyReal request patterns and who approvesPeople raise tickets differently from how the process says.
Interviews with ownersPurpose, business impact and the workaroundsPeople forget, and some things were set up by someone who has moved on.

Mapping dependencies

For every credential that something other than a person uses, I record who or what reads it, how (API call, agent, script, scheduled task or a hard-coded copy), how often, what stops working if the value changes, and who can fix it. That record decides the wave a credential goes into and whether it can be rotated at cutover. A hard-coded use is not a reason to skip the credential. It is a work item for its owner, with a date, before the wave.

Control equivalence

Before anything moves, every row of the capability map gets one decision. The matrix below is illustrative. The capabilities are typical, and the decisions are examples of the kind of result I record, not findings from a real estate.

Every gap ends in one of a short list of decisions: equivalent, better, a compensating control, an accepted gap with an owner and end date, or retired on purpose. A blank in the decision column blocks the checkpoint.

The shape of the migration

The plan has two halves. First, plan and prove: understand the old tool, discover, decide and sign off, then build a small proof in Dev. Then move and retire: rehearse in pre-production, go live in waves, rotate, and switch the old tool off. Rollback stays open across the second half.

The workflow, step by step

  1. Analyse the current tool

    What it does, what it fails to do, what it holds. Findings are written in a short document that the owners read and correct.

    GuardrailNo design before the current state is written downEvidenceCurrent-state analysis and capability map

  2. Run discovery and reconcile the inventory

    Compare the tool’s list with scans, logs, code and interviews. Every difference is either fixed or logged. Then each owner confirms their part of the list.

    GuardrailInventory signed off by owners before waves are plannedEvidenceReconciliation report and owner sign-offs

  3. Map dependencies

    For each non-human credential: who reads it, how, how often and what breaks. Hard-coded uses become tasks for their owners.

    GuardrailNo credential is rotated without a known list of readersEvidenceDependency register

  4. Decide control equivalence and sign off the plan

    Every capability row gets a decision. The migration plan, the processes around it and the wave plan go to the stakeholders for sign-off.

    GuardrailNo blank decisions at the checkpointEvidenceSigned control-equivalence matrix and migration plan

  5. Write and approve the foundations

    The password policy is reviewed and refined. Naming conventions for groups and roles are refined, approved and documented. Vaulting security is documented and approved by the business. Break glass is onboarded following organisation policy. Integrations with ServiceNow, Entra for SSO and MFA, and the SIEM are documented.

    GuardrailNothing is built before its policy is approvedEvidenceApproved policy, naming, vaulting and integration documents

  6. Prove it in Dev

    A small, limited proof with a few accounts from each class. The point is to find the surprises while they are cheap.

    GuardrailWritten go/no-go criteria to leave DevEvidenceProof results and sign-off

  7. Rehearse in pre-production and run in parallel

    Full-shape rehearsal, with integrations, failover and break glass exercised. The old tool keeps running for the accounts not yet moved.

    GuardrailBreak glass tested and high availability and disaster recovery exercised before go-liveEvidenceTest results, drill record

  8. Cut over in waves, then rotate

    Each wave has its own rollback criteria and an owner who accepts it. After acceptance, every secret in the wave is rotated and the old copy is retired.

    GuardrailRotation before the old vault is retiredEvidenceWave acceptance record, rotation history

  9. Decommission and keep the evidence

    Remove the old tool’s own privileged access to targets, archive its logs and session recordings, and switch it off on an agreed date.

    GuardrailNo switch-off until archive is readable and ownedEvidenceDecommission record, archive index

Example: a nightly job and a database login

This is an illustration, not a specific event.

What the inventory says

  • A database service account is owned by the infrastructure team.
  • Nobody has checked it out in a long time.
  • It looks safe to move early and rotate on day one.

What discovery shows and what I do

  • A scheduled job reads it through the old tool’s API every night.
  • I record the job and its owner, and ask the owner to repoint it to the target before the wave.
  • The account moves in the same wave as the job, and is rotated after the job has run once successfully.

Designing the waves

A wave is a set of accounts that move together, with a date, an owner and a way back. I order them using four criteria.

CriterionThe questionEffect on order
DependencyWhat reads this, and does it move in the same wave?Things that depend on each other go together.
RiskWhat is the exposure today, and what could a bad move cause?High exposure argues for early, but only once the route is proven.
Access criticalityHow badly does the business suffer if this access fails?The most critical access goes after a rehearsal, with an owner present.
Rollback readinessCan we go back, and have we tried?No wave without a tested way back.

In practice the first wave is small and low in criticality, so the team learns on something forgiving. Break glass is ready before the first production wave, because it is the way back in if something goes wrong.

Parallel running, cutover and rollback

Parallel running. Both tools are live for a while. The rule I keep is that one tool is the source of truth for any given account at any given time, and everybody knows which. Two tools each changing the same password is a recipe for lockouts.

Cutover. For each wave the accounts are verified in the target, the owners repoint their consumers, the old tool’s entries for those accounts go read-only, and the owner accepts the wave.

Rollback criteria. I write them before the wave, in plain language, with the decision maker named. Typical triggers:

  • A critical dependency cannot read its credential and cannot be fixed inside the agreed window.
  • Sessions cannot be brokered for a critical group of systems.
  • The approval or ticket integration fails, so access cannot be granted in a controlled way.
  • The break-glass test fails.

Rollback is simple until secrets are rotated, because the old vault still holds values that work. After rotation the old values are dead, so a rollback means restoring or re-setting them. For that reason I agree the point where the rollback window closes with the owners, and I put it right after acceptance and the first successful run of the dependent jobs.

Rotate everything at cutover

The old vault’s secrets should be treated as exposed to the old administrators. They could read them, the old backups hold them, and export files from the migration may have copies. Nobody needs to have done anything wrong for that to be true. It is a question of who could have seen them. So each secret is changed after it lands in the target, and then the export files and old copies are removed and recorded as removed. For people’s memorised passwords, NIST SP 800-63B advises against forced periodic changes without evidence of compromise. Vaulted privileged credentials are different: the tool sets them, nobody types them, and a change costs almost nothing.

High availability and disaster recovery

Everything now sits on cloud, and the platform will hold the keys to a lot. Before a production wave I want to know the target can lose a component without losing the service, that a second region exists with agreed recovery objectives, that a restore has actually been tried, and that break glass works when the platform or the identity provider does not. NIST SP 800-53 CP-10 expresses the same thing: recover the system to a known state within a time consistent with the recovery objectives.

Decommissioning and evidence retention

The old tool is not finished when the last wave moves. Its audit logs and session recordings often have to be kept for a retention period, so I plan who can read them, in what format, where they live and for how long.

  • Remove the old tool’s reach. It had accounts on targets to rotate and log in. Those accounts, and the integrations that used them, are removed.
  • Archive the evidence. Logs and recordings go to an archive with an owner, a read process and a retention date taken from the organisation’s own requirements. NIST SP 800-53 AU-11 asks for records to be retained for an organisation-defined period. Where PCI DSS applies, requirement 10.5.1 asks for at least 12 months of audit log history, with the most recent three months immediately available.
  • Check the archive can be read. Someone opens a sample before the old tool is switched off.
  • Switch off on an agreed date. Licences, backups, keys and exported files are handled and recorded.

  2. 2. Dev, a small proofA limited proof with accounts from each class, and the foundation documents drafted.Done whenThe written go/no-go criteria are met and the surprises are in the plan.
  3. 3. Pre-productionRehearsal with integrations, parallel running, failover and break glass exercised.Done whenTest results are accepted and the wave plan has dates and owners.
  4. 4. Production go-live in wavesCutover, acceptance and rotation for each wave, with rollback criteria.Done whenEvery in-scope account is accepted in the target, or sits on the exception register.

  • Weekly retrospective. Professional services, third parties and the customer meet every week to say what worked and what did not.
  • Weekly update to executives. A short progress report, so nothing is a surprise.
  • Handover gate. The runbook for each environment, the troubleshooting document and the operations document are approved before handover to the customer.

What tends to go wrong

  • Dependencies that nobody listed. A script, a report or a scheduled task uses a credential and only shows itself when the value changes.
  • Copying the mess. Orphaned accounts and stale owners move across unless someone decides they should not.
  • Gaps discovered at cutover. A behaviour of the old tool was never mapped, and the first time anyone notices is when it stops.
  • Parallel running with no end. Two tools stay live for so long that the old one becomes permanent.
  • Rollback that is assumed. Nobody tried it, and rotation has already made the old values useless.
  • Export files left behind. A file full of credentials on a share is a new breach waiting for a reason.
  • Logs lost with the old tool. The retention question gets asked after the switch-off.
  • A rushed sign-off. A signature without a walk-through is only a signature.

What I have learned to do differently

These are habits I keep. They are not a record of any single project.

  • I read the old tool as a source of requirements. What it does well becomes a requirement, and what it does badly tells me why the move was wanted.
  • I treat the inventory as a claim. I compare it with logs, scans and code before I believe it.
  • I walk owners through their part of the list. A conversation finds what an email never does.
  • I write the decision for every capability. Nothing is left as “we will see”.
  • I move in stages. Dev for a small proof, pre-production, then production. Each step has tests and a person who accepts the result.
  • I agree the rollback point up front. Everyone knows where it closes before the wave starts.
  • I rotate, and I remove the copies. Migration files count as secrets.
  • I plan the end of the old tool on day one. Archive, retention and switch-off are in the plan from the start.
  • I leave the runbooks behind. The team that runs the platform should not need me to keep it alive.
