Conditional Access, MFA and SSO

How to put sign-in rules in front of every app: one front door, proof that holds up against phishing, and rules that decide when to ask for more. Rolled out without locking anyone out.

Independent practitioner guide · Last reviewed 4 Nov 2026

A general guide from my own practice and public sources. Not a description of any employer’s systems, data or projects.

The short version

  • SSO gives you one place to make sign-in decisions. MFA is only as good as the weakest method a person is allowed to fall back to. Conditional access is the set of rules that chooses, at each sign-in, whether to allow, ask for more or block.
  • Start with admins and with the methods attackers already beat: SMS codes and push prompts a tired person accepts. Move admins to phishing-resistant methods (passkeys or security keys) first.
  • Every new rule runs in report-only mode first, and the emergency access accounts are excluded from every rule, so a mistake cannot lock out the people who fix mistakes.
  • The rollout fails at recovery, not at sign-in. Plan the help desk reset path before you switch anything on.

The problem

Most organisations have MFA “on” and still lose accounts. The usual reasons: some apps bypass the identity provider, the MFA method can be phished or fatigued, legacy sign-in protocols skip MFA entirely, and exceptions granted during the rollout are never removed.

The terms, in plain words

  • SSO (single sign-on): apps trust one identity provider, so a person signs in once and the provider decides.
  • MFA: more than one kind of proof. Not all kinds are equal.
  • Phishing-resistant MFA: the method is bound to the real site, so a fake page cannot relay it. Passkeys and security keys (FIDO2/WebAuthn) are the common examples.
  • Conditional access: rules that look at who, which app, which device, where and how risky the sign-in is, then allow, require more, or block.
  • Report-only mode: the rule is evaluated and logged, but not enforced.

What the standards say

Checked on 4 November 2026.

  • NIST SP 800-63B-4: verifiers “SHALL offer at least one phishing-resistant authentication option at AAL2”, and AAL3 “requires a phishing-resistant authenticator”. Use of the phone network (SMS or voice) for out-of-band verification “is restricted”.
  • CISA’s guidance (October 2022) pushes organisations to phishing-resistant MFA, and to number matching as an interim step for push-based apps.
  • Microsoft’s Conditional Access planning guide: run each policy in report-only mode “for at least one week before enforcement”, and exclude emergency access accounts “to prevent lockout due to policy misconfiguration”.

The rollout, in risk order

  1. Inventory the front doors. List every app and how it signs people in: through the identity provider, a local login, or a legacy protocol. Apps outside SSO are outside every rule you write.
  2. Protect the emergency accounts first. Two cloud-only emergency accounts, excluded from every rule, with strong credentials stored offline and an alert on every use. The break-glass case study covers this in full.
  3. Admins to phishing-resistant MFA. Passkeys or security keys for every privileged role, with no SMS fallback.
  4. Block legacy sign-in. Old protocols that cannot do MFA are where a correct policy is quietly bypassed.
  5. Everyone to MFA, with number matching or better. Then move groups to passkeys in risk order: finance, HR, engineers with production access.
  6. Add context rules. Require a managed device for sensitive apps, step up for risky sign-ins, block countries you never operate in (emergency accounts still excluded).
  7. Remove the exceptions. Every exclusion has an owner and an end date, and is reviewed.

How I name and test rules

  • A name that says number, app, response, who and when, so a rule can be found on a call: for example CA03 - All apps: Require phishing-resistant MFA - Admins - Always.
  • Report-only for at least a week, then read the sign-in logs for who would have been blocked.
  • A written test plan with expected results, run with test users, including the exclusions.
  • Rollback is a switch: disable the rule, or exclude one person for a short, recorded time.

The recovery problem

Strong sign-in moves the attack to the help desk. An attacker with a name and a phone number asks for a reset. Before the rollout, put three things in place: identity proof that does not rely on things an attacker can look up, a notice to the person whenever their methods change, and a short delay on admin resets.

Worked example (made up)

Kaveri Finance is invented: 1,200 staff, 60 admins, 140 apps. 18 apps sign in locally and are outside SSO. Admins use push MFA, and 4 of them accepted prompts they did not start in one month.

  • Week 1: emergency accounts set up and excluded; admin passkey rule in report-only. Logs show 3 service tools that would break.
  • Week 2: the 3 tools moved to workload identities; admin rule switched on.
  • Weeks 3 to 6: legacy sign-in blocked; number matching for all; finance and HR move to passkeys.
  • Still open: the 18 local-login apps, each with an owner and a date.

Where conditional access usually fails

  • Rules that are correct but never apply, because the app is not behind SSO.
  • An exclusion group that grows every month.
  • SMS kept “just for recovery”, which becomes the way in.
  • No alert when an emergency account signs in.
  • Policies changed in the console with no record of who changed what.

What an auditor will ask

  • Which apps are behind SSO, and which are not?
  • Which accounts are excluded from which rules, and why?
  • How are admins authenticated, and can they fall back to a weaker method?
  • Show a policy change and its approval.

Habits I now keep

  • Report-only first, every time.
  • Emergency accounts excluded and alerted, every rule.
  • No exception without an owner and an end date.
