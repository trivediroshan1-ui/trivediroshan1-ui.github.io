Break-Glass Emergency Access

Designing emergency access that stays usable under pressure without becoming a permanent bypass.

Credential Management Platform Owner & PAM Product Owner · Last reviewed 1 Oct 2026

The short version

  • A break-glass account is for the day normal access has failed. It has to work when the PAM platform, the identity provider and the MFA service may all be down, so it cannot lean on any of them.
  • Five habits keep it from turning into a side door: credentials kept apart from standing accounts, every use a logged event, an alert on any sign-in or checkout, a written justification, and rotation the same day.
  • Test it on a calendar. An emergency account nobody has tried since it was set up is a guess, and a guess is a poor control.

The problem

Emergency access exists to keep incident response moving. Without controls on when it could be invoked, break-glass credentials were also the easiest path around normal approval, and nobody could tell the difference after the fact.

The design has to hold up against two opposite failures. If emergency access is hard to reach, people lose hours during an outage, or they quietly keep a shared admin login that nobody governs. If it is easy to reach and quiet to use, it becomes the route people take whenever approval feels slow. Three properties cover both: it works when everything else is down, it is loud when it is used, and every use is closed out afterwards.

The kinds of break-glass

“Break-glass” is not one account. Each layer that can lock people out needs its own way back in, and each has a different owner and a different fallback.

TypeWhat it gets you back intoWhy it stands apart
Identity provider or cloud tenant emergency accountsAdministration of the tenant when normal admins cannot sign inCloud-only, not federated and not synchronized, so an outage of federation or of the on-premises directory does not touch it
PAM platform recoveryThe vault and its admin console when the platform is degraded or its own sign-in is brokenA vault cannot hold the only key to itself. The recovery credential lives outside it, in sealed storage
Infrastructure local adminServers, hypervisors and databases when directory sign-in failsA local account on the machine works even if the directory does not. It is normally vaulted and rotated, with a sealed copy for the day the vault is unreachable
Directory and domainThe directory every other system trusts, so it can be repaired or restoredEverything else signs in through it, so its recovery path cannot sign in through it
Network devicesFirewalls, switches and routers when central authentication is unreachableLocal or console accounts, used over a management path that does not cross the network being repaired

Keep the list short. Every extra emergency account is another spare key, and every one needs an owner, a reason and a drill.

Do not depend on what might be down

The question I ask about every emergency route is: what has to be working for this to work? If the answer includes the thing that just failed, the route is not an emergency route.

Sealed fallback storage

When the vault itself cannot be reached, the credentials that restart the chain come from sealed, offline storage. The aim is that opening it takes two people and leaves a trace.

  • Split custody or dual control. Either two halves of the secret are held by two people, or two keys are needed to open the container. No single person can use it alone.
  • Tamper-evident and in separate places. Microsoft’s guidance calls for fireproof safes in secure, separate locations, with combinations changed regularly and whenever someone with access leaves.
  • Opening it is an event. It goes in a custody log with names, time and incident reference, and it triggers the same close-out as any other use: justification, rotation, a fresh sealed copy.
  • Only what restarts the chain. The sealed set holds what is needed to get the platform and the identity layer back, and nothing more.
  • Kept in step with the vault. A sealed copy that no longer matches the live secret fails at the worst moment. Refreshing it is part of rotation, and the drill checks it.

Naming and protection

  • A naming convention, approved and written down. It makes clear that the account is an emergency account, so alert rules, reports and reviewers recognise it straight away. It is tied to a role and a purpose. It is not tied to a person.
  • No link to an individual. Microsoft’s guidance says not to associate these accounts with any individual user, not to connect them to employee-supplied devices such as phones, and to make sure nobody has registered MFA or self-service password reset to a personal device.
  • Two or more accounts, cloud-only. For the tenant, the guidance says to create two or more emergency access accounts, use the onmicrosoft.com domain, and keep them unfederated and unsynchronized.
  • Phishing-resistant sign-in that differs from daily admins. The guidance recommends a passkey (FIDO2) and offers certificate-based authentication where a PKI already exists. It adds that the method should differ from the one the normal administrator accounts use.
  • Permanent and active, with no expiry. In Privileged Identity Management the guidance asks for the Global Administrator assignment to be permanent active, not eligible. It also says the credential must not expire or fall under automated clean-up for lack of use, which matters for an account that is dormant by design.
  • A designated secure workstation. The guidance asks that people using these accounts do so from a designated secure workstation or a privileged access workstation.

The mechanism

This is the cycle every break-glass credential goes through. The credential sleeps in the vault. An incident wakes it, it is used under watch, and it is put back to sleep with a new secret.

One use, start to finish

  1. Declare the emergency

    A person in a named role says that normal access has failed and gives the reason, with an incident reference. Nobody is handed break-glass access because they asked politely.

    GuardrailOnly named roles may declareEvidenceIncident reference and stated reason

  2. Approve, or take the fast path

    Normally a named approver says yes, and the credential is released. If the approval tooling is itself down, the fast path applies: two custodians open the sealed copy together, and the approval is recorded as soon as tooling is back.

    GuardrailFast path needs two peopleEvidenceApproval record or custody log entry

  3. Invoke

    The checkout or the unsealing is a distinct, logged event. It is never a quiet reuse of a standing account. Any sign-in or checkout raises an alert to the on-call person and to security.

    GuardrailAlert on any useEvidenceAlert record and log entry

  4. Use it, under watch

    The person works from a secure workstation, stays inside the incident, and the session is recorded where the platform can record it. Where it cannot, the person keeps a timed note of each action so the review has something to check against the system logs.

    GuardrailUse limited to the incident and a time boxEvidenceSession recording or timed action notes

  5. Close out with a justification

    The person writes down why normal access was not enough, what was done and what the outcome was. A missing justification becomes an open finding that somebody chases.

    GuardrailNo justification, open findingEvidenceJustification linked to the incident

  6. Rotate and return

    The credential is rotated the same day. The new secret is checked with a test sign-in, the sealed copy is refreshed, and the credential goes back to the vault dormant until the next incident.

    GuardrailSame-day rotation, then a test sign-inEvidenceRotation record with time

  7. Review within a fixed time

    Someone who did not use the credential reads the logs against the justification within the agreed window. An advisory check flags usage that matches normal-hours, repeat-user patterns, so the reviewer looks hardest where use looks routine and not urgent. The check advises and a person decides.

    GuardrailReviewer is independent of the userEvidenceSigned review with findings

Alerting, and checking the alerts work

Microsoft’s guidance is to configure alerts for every use of an emergency access account and to capture sign-in and audit logs for review. Its worked example uses Azure Monitor with a threshold of greater than zero and critical severity, and it says Microsoft Sentinel or other tools can do the same job and send email and SMS to administrators. The same rule applies to the other types: any checkout, unsealing or sign-in raises an alert.

An alert that has quietly stopped is worse than none, because people believe they are covered. So I add a second layer that watches the first:

  • A scheduled test event. The drill sign-in, or a synthetic one, must produce an alert. If the alert does not arrive, that is a finding, raised the same day.
  • A missing-heartbeat rule. If the expected test event is not seen in the log store, something upstream has broken, and that raises its own alert.
  • More than one route. Alerts go out over channels that do not share a failure mode, such as email and SMS, and they do not rely only on the systems that might be down.
  • Logs outside the platform. Records go to a store that the platform’s own admins cannot edit.
  • Security monitoring knows about drills. Microsoft’s validation steps include making sure monitoring staff are aware when account-check activity is under way, so a drill does not start a real incident.

Drills: how often, and what they prove

For the tenant accounts, Microsoft’s guidance says to validate at regular intervals and for key changes, at least every 90 days, for example every quarter. It also names triggers such as a change in IT staff or a change in the organization’s subscriptions. I use the same rhythm for the other types, as my own choice and not as something that document prescribes.

A drill checksIf it fails, you learn

A drill shows that the route works. It says nothing about whether incident response itself is any good, and I keep those two exercises separate.

Onboarding the process

I treat break-glass as a process to be onboarded under the organisation’s policy, the same as any other privileged access. That means:

  • The password policy is reviewed and refined for these accounts, which are used rarely and by people under pressure.
  • Naming conventions are approved and documented.
  • Vaulting security is documented, with business approval.
  • High availability and disaster recovery are considered from the start. When everything runs in the cloud, the emergency route must not sit on the same dependency as the thing it recovers.
  • The process, the custody list and the drill record are written down where the next person will find them.

Example: the sign-in service fails in the night

This is an illustration of the process, not a specific event.

During

  • The identity provider is down. Admins cannot sign in to the PAM platform, which uses that provider.
  • The on-call engineer declares the incident and gives the reason.
  • The approval tool sits behind the same provider, so the fast path applies. Two custodians open the sealed recovery credential together and log it.
  • An alert reaches the on-call person and security as soon as the credential is used.
  • The engineer restores sign-in from a secure workstation, and the session is recorded.

Afterwards

  • The engineer writes the justification: what failed, what was done, what was changed.
  • The recovery credential is rotated the same day, tested with a sign-in and returned to sealed storage with a fresh copy.
  • A reviewer who was not involved checks the log against the justification within the agreed window.
  • The custody log, alert, recording, rotation record and review are kept together as the evidence for that use.

What tends to go wrong

  • It works on day one and is never tried again. A policy change, a departed keyholder or a clean-up job breaks it, and nobody finds out until the emergency.
  • The recovery credential is inside the thing it recovers. The vault holds the only copy of the vault’s own recovery secret.
  • Clean-up jobs disable the dormant account. An account with no sign-ins looks stale to a tidy-up script unless it is excluded on purpose.
  • The alert goes nowhere. It lands in a mailbox nobody watches, or the route broke and no test noticed.
  • The fast path becomes the normal path. If approval is slow, people reach for the glass. The advisory check is there to catch that pattern, and slow approval is the thing to fix.
  • Sealed copies drift. The vault secret is rotated and the sealed copy is left behind.
  • Custody goes stale. People leave or change roles and the list, the combinations and the training are not updated.
  • Too many accounts with too much power. Each extra account is one more thing to watch.
  • Everything on one cloud dependency. The platform and the route to recover it can fail together if they share a region or a provider service.
  • A drill that only reads the procedure. Nobody signs in, nobody opens the seal, and the drill proves nothing.

What I have learned to do differently

These are habits I keep, not a record of any one project.

  • I draw the dependencies first. Before I design an emergency route I write down what it needs in order to work, then ask whether any of that could be down at the same time.
  • I write the process down and get it approved before I build. Policy, naming and vaulting security are documented and signed off by the business, so the design survives a change of people.
  • I review the password policy for these accounts. A rule written for daily users does not suit a secret that is used rarely, by someone in a hurry, and kept sealed in between.
  • I design for the platform being down. High availability and recovery are part of the first design, and I check that the emergency route does not share a dependency with what it recovers.
  • I put the drill on a calendar and tell monitoring beforehand. A drill that is not scheduled does not happen.
  • I fix a slow approval path instead of tolerating the workaround. When people reach for break-glass to skip a queue, the queue is the defect.
  • I keep the advisory check advisory. It points a reviewer at use that looks routine. A person makes the call.
  • I keep the list short. Every break-glass account has an owner, a reason and a drill, or it is retired.
