Access Recertification & IGA Automation

Turning a manual quarterly access review into a governed process backed by usage evidence, so that a reviewer’s “keep” means something.

Credential Management Platform Owner · Last reviewed 1 Oct 2026

The short version

  • A quarterly review that asks managers to approve long lists from memory produces a signature, not a control. Dormant access survives every cycle.
  • Give the reviewer evidence on every line: who owns the account, whether it was used, and an advisory keep or revoke suggestion they can overrule. Then let the decision execute from the identity governance system, and check that it worked.
  • A certification is only finished when the access is gone and you can show it. Everything before that is paperwork.

The problem

Access certification, or recertification, is the periodic check that the people who hold access should still hold it. On paper it is simple. Every quarter, each manager gets a list of what their people can reach, and ticks keep or revoke.

In practice the list is long and the manager does not know what half the entries are. “SG-FIN-RW-04” tells them nothing. They cannot remember whether the person still uses it, so they do the sensible thing under time pressure and approve it all. The campaign closes on schedule, the audit file shows the campaign complete, and the dormant access is still there next quarter. The review became a rubber stamp instead of a control.

The weak point is rarely the tool that sends the emails. It is the quality of what the reviewer sees and what happens after they answer. Four things tend to be missing:

  • Context. Entitlement names that only an administrator can read, with no hint of what they allow.
  • Evidence. No sign of whether the access was ever used, so “keep” is the safe guess.
  • The right reviewer. Managers asked about application roles they have never seen, and nobody asked about the accounts that have no manager at all.
  • Follow-through. A revoke decision that turns into a ticket, which waits in a queue, and is closed without anyone looking at the target system.

How it fits together

The flow has seven steps, and the same few records travel through all of them. Account and entitlement data comes in from the directory, the applications and the HR feed, and sits next to usage logs. A risk tier decides how often each account is reviewed and by whom. The reviewer sees the line with its evidence and a suggestion. The decision goes back into the identity governance system, which executes it, and a separate check confirms the access is gone.

Two choices in this design carry most of the weight. The first is that usage data is joined to entitlement data before the reviewer sees anything, so evidence is part of the question and not a lookup the reviewer has to do. The second is that the identity governance system stays the system of record for the decision and its execution. A revoke does not leave that system as an email asking someone to do something.

Choosing the reviewer

“The manager reviews everything” is the default, and it is wrong for a lot of access. A manager knows whether a person still needs access to the team’s shared drive. They usually cannot judge a database role, an admin console permission or a finance approval limit.

What is being reviewedWho should reviewWhy

Application roles with business meaningApplication or data ownerThey know what the role allows and who ought to hold it

A reviewer should not approve their own access, and should not be the only person who approved it in the first place. The tool can enforce both. Where it cannot, the line is routed to a second reviewer.

One review cycle, step by step

  1. Collect entitlements into one inventory

    Each connected system sends its accounts and entitlements. They are matched to a person or an owner, and stamped with the last time the access was used. Where a system cannot report usage, the line says “no usage data” in plain words, so the reviewer knows the evidence is missing and does not read silence as activity.

    GuardrailEvery account maps to a person or a named owner, or it is flaggedEvidenceInventory snapshot with collection time

  2. Tier by risk and set the frequency

    Not every line deserves the same attention. Privileged access, access to sensitive data and anything that can move money are reviewed more often than a read-only role on an internal wiki. The tier is set once by security, written down, and applied by the system, so a team cannot choose a gentler schedule for itself.

    GuardrailFrequency follows tier, and tiers are set by securityEvidenceTier policy and the tier on each line

  3. Pick the reviewer and build the pack

    The system assigns the reviewer from the table above, not from a single manager field. Each line shows a readable description of what the entitlement allows, when it was granted, who approved it, and when it was last used. The pack is as short as the tier allows. Low-risk access that has been used recently is grouped so the reviewer can confirm it in one action.

    GuardrailNo self-review, and a second reviewer where the owner is the holderEvidenceReviewer assignment record

  4. Review with usage evidence and the advisory flag

    Entitlements with no use in the agreed window are pre-flagged as likely revokes, with the reason shown: “not used in the last N days”. The reviewer confirms or overrides each flag. Starting from a suggestion with a reason is faster than starting from a blank line, and it makes agreement a visible choice. Any model that produces the flag is advisory only, and its inputs are the usage and ownership data already in the inventory.

    GuardrailThe flag is a suggestion. Overriding it needs a short reason, and so does approving a line with no usageEvidenceFlag, reviewer action and reason, per line

  5. Record the decision

    Every line ends as keep, revoke or change, with the reviewer, the time and the evidence that was on screen when they chose. A campaign cannot be closed with open lines. Lines nobody answers escalate to the reviewer’s manager after a set time, and after a second deadline the default is to suspend the access for review, not to keep it.

    GuardrailNo answer is never an implicit approvalEvidenceDecision log with timestamps and escalations

  6. Execute revocations from the system of record

    Approved revokes are pushed by the identity governance system to the target system through its connector, or raised as a task to the owning team where no connector exists. The decision and the execution stay on one record, so nobody has to re-key a list from a spreadsheet. Where removal has a business impact, the change goes through the normal change process first.

    GuardrailA revoke has a due date, and an overdue revoke escalatesEvidenceRevocation request and its completion time

  7. Verify and report

    After execution, the system reads the target again and confirms the entitlement is gone. A revoke that did not take effect is reopened. Reporting then covers what was reviewed, what was changed, what was overridden, what is still open and who signed off. The report is built from the logs, not typed up afterward.

    GuardrailDone means verified in the target systemEvidenceRe-read result and the sign-off record

Example: one reviewer, one quarter

This is an illustration, not a specific event. The counts are made up to show the shape of the work.

Before

  • A manager of eight people gets one spreadsheet with every entitlement, named by technical ID.
  • Nothing shows which lines were used.
  • They approve all of it on the last day, because rejecting something they do not understand feels risky.
  • Revokes, if any, go to a service desk queue by email.

After

  • The manager sees plain descriptions, the last-used date and a flag on lines unused for the agreed window.
  • Grouped low-risk lines are confirmed together. The flagged lines get individual attention.
  • An override of a flag needs a sentence, so “keep” on an unused line is a considered choice.
  • Approved revokes execute from the governance system, and the re-read shows them gone.

Orphaned, dormant, privileged and service accounts

The ordinary employee account is the easy case. The ones that keep surfacing in audits are the accounts the standard flow does not fit.

  • Dormant accounts exist and are valid, but nothing has used them for a long time. A review that shows last use catches them, and a rule that disables them after an agreed period of inactivity catches them between reviews. The review should confirm what the rule has done, not be the only thing that does it.
  • Orphaned accounts have no active owner: the person left, the team was reorganized, the system changed hands. They come out of the inventory step as lines with no person to match. They go to the platform owner, who either assigns an owner within a deadline or disables the account.
  • Privileged accounts get the shortest cycle and a stricter reviewer path. The questions are whether the person still needs this level, and whether the privilege is standing or can be requested when needed. Reviews find the standing access, and the answer is often to move it to request-and-approve rather than keep it.
  • Service accounts have no manager, no leaving date and often no obvious owner. They stay in the same review, with an owner recorded at creation, a purpose, and a note of where the credential is used. The owner is asked to confirm the account still has a job. An account whose owner cannot be found goes onto the orphan list.
  • Movers carry old access into a new role. The review is a backstop. The better control is removing access when the person moves, which is why the HR feed sits at the source end of the diagram.

Keeping the review from becoming a stamp

Evidence helps, but people still find ways to click through. These are the habits I design against.

  • Short packs. A reviewer handed hundreds of lines will approve hundreds of lines. Group the low-risk, recently used access, and spend attention on the rest.
  • Visible agreement. If the reviewer approves a line the system flagged as unused, they give a reason. It takes seconds and it changes behavior.
  • Watch the pattern. A reviewer who approves everything in a few minutes, campaign after campaign, is a finding in itself. Reviewers see their own override and revoke counts, and the platform owner sees the outliers.
  • No silent defaults. Unanswered lines escalate and then suspend. Waiting it out is not allowed to be a way to keep access.
  • Spot checks. Security samples a few “keep” decisions each cycle, asks the reviewer why, and checks the answer against the usage record.
  • Respect the reviewer’s time. Review dates are announced early, reminders go out on a schedule, and the pack is ready when the campaign opens. A rushed review is a rubber stamp by design.

  2. 2. Build the inventoryConnect the highest-risk systems first, along with the HR feed, and map every account to a person or an owner.Done whenThe inventory exists, and the orphaned accounts in it are listed, not hidden.
  3. 3. Make the lines readableReplace technical entitlement names with plain descriptions, and add last-used data where systems can supply it.Done whenA manager can say what a line allows without asking an administrator.
  4. 4. Run a pilot campaignOne tier and a small set of reviewers, with the advisory flag on. Ask reviewers what confused them.Done whenReviewers can finish without help, and the pilot’s revokes were executed and verified.

  • Reviewers answer for their decisions. A keep decision on unused access carries the reviewer’s name and reason.
  • Measure the quality of the review, not just completion. Useful measures: revokes verified as done, time to execute, lines closed by escalation, overrides of the advisory flag, and accounts with no owner.
  • Exceptions have owners and end dates. A system that cannot supply usage data, or an account that cannot be disabled, goes on a register that is reviewed on a rhythm.

What tends to go wrong

  • The data is worse than expected. Entitlement exports are inconsistent, owners are missing, and some systems report no usage at all. Say so in the pack instead of hiding it.
  • Usage is a hint and not a verdict. Access held for a quarterly close, a disaster recovery role or an annual task can look dormant. The flag needs to be overridable, and reviewers need room to explain.
  • Reviewer fatigue returns. Once the novelty fades, people click through again. Keep packs short, and keep watching the approval patterns.
  • Revokes break something. Removing access that a process quietly depended on causes an incident and a loss of trust. Higher-impact removals go through change control, and a restore path exists.
  • Connectors take time. Some systems have no clean way to remove access automatically. Those stay as tracked tasks with owners until they can be connected.
  • The advisory step gets treated as the decision. If reviewers start accepting every flag without reading, the control has moved to a model nobody is accountable for. Keep a human reason on record.

What I have learned to do differently

These are habits I keep when I design or run a certification process. They are not a record of one project.

  • Start by reading a real review pack as the reviewer. I open one and try to answer it. If I cannot tell what a line allows, neither can they, and the tool is not the problem.
  • Fix the data before the workflow. A polished campaign built on a bad inventory produces confident wrong answers. I spend the first stage on the inventory and on owners.
  • Treat the follow-through as part of the review. I do not count a campaign as complete until the revokes are verified. The completion rate that matters is on removals.
  • Give reviewers a reason to trust the flag. I show why a line is flagged, and I let them overrule it without friction. A suggestion that cannot be argued with gets ignored or obeyed blindly.
  • Pilot with the people who will complain. A small group of skeptical reviewers finds the confusing wording and the missing data faster than a sponsor does.
  • Agree the reviewer rules with the business first. Who reviews what, and how long they have, is written down and approved before the first campaign. It saves arguments later.
  • Keep privileged and service accounts in the same cycle. It is tempting to handle them separately because they are awkward. I bring them in early, with their own reviewer path, so they do not become a permanent exception.
