PAM, NHI & Cloud Platform Evaluation

How I compare privileged access, non-human identity and cloud security platforms: written scenarios, the same test for every tool, and a decision someone can audit later.

Evaluation and PoC lead · Last reviewed 1 Oct 2026

The short version

  • Feature lists do not settle a platform decision. A scenario that one tool handles and another only half handles does.
  • I write the scenarios, weights and pass marks first, run every candidate through the same scripts, and score before anyone argues.
  • The result is a decision record that links what the tool can do to integration, operations, audit and who will own it.

The problem

The markets for privileged access, non-human identity (NHI) and cloud security move fast. Tools overlap, names change and every demo looks good. A feature checklist rewards the vendor with the longest list, not the one that fits the organization.

The harder part is that the people in the room want different things. Security wants control, engineers want speed, audit wants evidence and finance wants a number. A decision made from demos alone tends to favor whoever presented last. I want a method that survives those disagreements and can be explained a year later.

The order I follow

It starts with people, not products. I talk to stakeholders in different business units to understand their risks, how security affects their users, what compliance and audit need from them, what infrastructure they run, which regions they work in and how the organization is structured. That becomes a written pain list. Then I recommend proofs of concept with different tools, look at the results and decide which to buy. Once licenses and tools are procured, professional services take over the build.

What this covered

  • Scenario-led assessments built around identity types, trust boundaries and lifecycle responsibilities.
  • Proof-of-concept work on Delinea Secret Server and Privileged Remote Access, focused on practical use cases.
  • Product analysis and operating-model planning for StrongDM, alongside structured assessments of Clutch Security and Wiz.

This page is about the method. It does not compare or rank those products, and nothing here is a statement about what any of them can or cannot do. For that, read the vendor’s own documentation and run your own scenarios.

The scenario catalog

I build the catalog around identity types, because each one has a different trust boundary and a different lifecycle owner. Each row becomes a test script. The scenarios are examples of the shape, not a complete list.

SSH keyA shared deployment key sits on many serversFinding where it is, replacing it with per-user or short-lived access, removing the old key
Cloud workloadA container needs a database login at start-upFetching a short-lived credential at runtime with no secret baked into the image

AI agentAn agent calls an internal API on a user’s behalfWhose identity it acts under, how narrow the access is, how it is stopped, and whether its actions are logged

Weighted criteria

Scenario results are one input. I group the rest into nine criteria and agree the weights with the sponsor before the first demo. A few items are gates that a tool must pass regardless of its score, such as a data-residency requirement. Gates are pass or fail and sit outside the weighting.

CriterionWhat I askExample evidence
Security controlsDoes it enforce least privilege, approval, session control and secret protection in our scenarios?POC script results
CoverageHow many of our scenarios does it handle directly, and how many need a workaround?Scenario catalog, marked per tool
IntegrationDoes it work with our service desk, directory and SIEM without custom glue?Integration test with ServiceNow, Entra and the SIEM
High availability and DRWhat happens to access when it is down, and how fast can it be restored?Outage test and recovery notes
Deployment modelDoes SaaS, self-hosted or hybrid fit our regions and data rules?Architecture review
Operating model and skillsWho runs it, what skills do they need, and how much daily effort does it take?Run-book draft and effort estimate
Audit evidenceCan I answer who, what, when and approved by whom, from the tool itself?Sample reports and log export
Licensing and total costHow is it priced, and what does it cost once services, training and support are in?Quote with assumptions written down
Vendor viability, roadmap and supportWill the vendor and the product be here in five years, and how do they handle problems?Due-diligence pack, references, support terms

The evaluation, step by step

  1. Listen and write the pain list

    Interviews across the business units, then a one-page list of the risks, user frictions, audit needs and regional or infrastructure limits. The sponsor signs it off.

    GuardrailPain list agreed before tools are namedEvidenceInterview notes, signed list

  2. Write scenarios, weights and gates

    Pick the scenarios from the catalog that match the pain list, set the weights and the gates, and write pass marks for each script step.

    GuardrailCriteria dated before the first demoEvidenceCriteria document

  3. Design the proof of concept

    Success criteria in writing before starting. A fixed time box, a test environment with no real secrets, a named person who runs each test, and the same scripts for every tool. The scripts include failure cases: the vault is down, a rotation fails, an access grant is revoked mid-session.

    GuardrailSame scripts, same time box, test data onlyEvidencePOC plan, environment review

  4. Run it and log everything

    Each tool is set up and tested by the same people where possible. Every bit of vendor help is logged, because help during a trial may not exist in production.

    GuardrailVendor assistance recordedEvidenceRun log, support log

  5. Score on your own first

    Each scorer fills in a sheet alone, then we compare. Where scores differ by two points or more, we go back to the evidence and not to opinion.

    GuardrailIndependent scoring before discussionEvidenceIndividual score sheets

  6. Check the vendor

    Due diligence runs alongside the trial, covered in the next section. A strong trial does not cancel a weak answer here.

    GuardrailSecurity review before production accessEvidenceDue-diligence pack

  7. Write the decision record

    What was chosen and why, the scores, the trade-offs, the risks accepted and who accepted them, and any dissent.

    GuardrailRisk acceptance namedEvidenceSigned decision record

  8. Hand over

    Once licenses are bought, the statement of work, the engagement letter, the training program and support terms go to professional services. The scenarios and scripts become acceptance tests for the build, and the POC findings feed the architecture.

    GuardrailPOC scripts reused as acceptance testsEvidenceSOW, training plan, architecture inputs

Designing the proof of concept

  • Success criteria first. For each script, write what a pass, a partial and a fail look like before anyone touches a tool.
  • One script, every tool. If a script has to change for one tool, it changes for all of them and the earlier runs are repeated.
  • A fixed time box. Two weeks per tool is an example. The number matters less than giving every candidate the same.
  • A test environment that looks like ours. Same network zones, same directory integration and similar target systems, with test credentials only.
  • A named runner. Someone from our side runs the tests. Vendor staff can advise, and that is logged.
  • Failure cases on purpose. Take the vault offline, break a rotation, revoke access while a session is open, lose the connection to the directory. The way a tool fails says more than the way it works.

Candidate B’s happy path was fine. The failure step is where the scores separated, which is why failure cases are in every script. A scenario score of 3 means the pass mark was met with no workaround, 2 needed a manual step, 1 needed significant effort, 0 could not be done.

Adding it up, and testing the weights

Weighted points are the weight times the 0 to 3 score, summed. Four criteria are shown for brevity.

Two things to notice. The totals are close, so the total alone cannot decide anything. And a small change to the weights reorders the candidates. Whenever the order moves that easily, I do not treat the number as the answer. I go back to the scenarios and the gates, and I ask the sponsor which weights they really believe.

Keeping bias out

  • Write first. Criteria and weights come before the demos, so they cannot be shaped around a favorite.
  • Score alone, then talk. Early opinions anchor the room.
  • Rotate the order. Do not always run the same tool first or last, and note who presented when.
  • Treat vendor effort as data. If a vendor engineer configured something for us, the operating-model score reflects that we could not have done it ourselves on day one.
  • Test the weights. Move them around as above, and see whether the decision survives.
  • Bring in someone who did not attend. A reader from audit or security architecture who reads only the record, and asks what is missing.

Security due diligence on the vendor

The product may be good and still be a poor thing to trust with your most privileged credentials. I ask for, and read, the following before any production access is granted:

  • Certifications and audit reports. Which reports, what scope, what dates, and whether the product I am buying sits inside that scope.
  • Penetration-test summaries. Recent, independent, with findings and how they were closed.
  • Data residency. Where data and backups live, where support staff can access it from, and which sub-processors are involved.
  • Vulnerability handling. How issues are reported, how fast patches arrive and how customers are told.
  • Exit and data export. How I get my secrets, policies and audit logs out in a usable form, and what happens to my data when the contract ends.
  • Viability and support. Support hours and escalation, references I can call, and what the roadmap commits to in writing.

Open questions are written into the decision record. An unanswered question is a risk that someone has to accept by name.

  • The decision belongs to the business. I prepare the analysis and the recommendation. The sponsor decides.
  • Someone owns the product afterwards. The evaluation names the product owner and the platform team before contracts are signed, so ownership does not become a question after go-live.

What tends to go wrong

  • The decision is already made. If a tool was chosen informally, the POC becomes theatre. It is better to say so and adjust the scope.
  • Demos replace tests. A polished demo shows a scripted path. Only your own scripts show yours.
  • The POC is too easy. A clean test network hides directory quirks, proxy rules and legacy systems that decide the real effort.
  • The happy path wins. Without failure cases, a tool that fails badly can still score well.
  • Total cost is missed. Licenses are one line. Services, training, infrastructure, support and the people to run it add up.
  • Scope drifts to a bigger product. Cloud security, NHI and PAM overlap. Decide which problem you are solving, or the comparison stops being fair.
  • Handover is forgotten. The POC learning sits in one person’s head and professional services start from nothing.

What I have learned to do differently

These are habits I keep, not a record of any one project.

  • I write the pass marks before I see the tool. It is much harder to be fair afterwards.
  • I test how things fail. I put the outage and the failed rotation in every script.
  • I keep vendor help in a log. It shows the real effort of running the tool later.
  • I treat the score as a prompt for questions. When totals are close I go back to the scenarios and the evidence.
  • I write the exit plan during the evaluation. It is easier to ask about data export before a contract than after.
  • I involve the operators early. The people who will run the platform estimate the effort, not the people who will only buy it.
  • I hand over the scripts. Reused as acceptance tests, they tie the purchase to what we were promised.
