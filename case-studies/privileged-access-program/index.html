Method · Case study

How I run a privileged access program, from the first conversation to the last training session

Start with the organization’s pain, not with the tool. This is the order I follow and the documents I leave behind.

Architecture and product lead · Last reviewed 1 Oct 2026

The short version

  • Privilege is not only about people. It also covers service accounts and other non-human identities, IoT, operational technology and now AI agents.
  • An architect has to understand the organization’s first pain before anything else. That starts with talking to people, not with a product demo.
  • The tool is chosen after a proof of concept, and the design, inventory and plans are written down so the next person can pick them up.

What “privileged” covers

Privilege is not limited to human accounts, or to accounts tied to an identity that has been granted elevated rights. It comes in several kinds: human administrators, service accounts and other non-human identities (NHI), IoT devices, operational technology (OT), and now AI agents.

The same goes for credentials. A program that only looks at passwords misses API tokens, SSH keys, API keys, dynamic secrets and static secrets. All of them open doors, so all of them need an owner and a lifecycle.

Start with the pain

Before I think about tools, I want to know what the organization is struggling with today. I look at four things together:

  • The risks it faces now. Where could a stolen or misused account hurt the business most?
  • The effect on users. Where does security slow people down, so they work around it?
  • Compliance and audit. What does the firm have to prove, and to whom?
  • The business impact. Weak security and heavy friction both cost money, through incidents and through lost revenue.

Talk to people first

I get the picture by talking to stakeholders in different business units. I want to understand the organization chart and structure, the regions the business operates in, the major risks they see, and the infrastructure they run. Much of what a good design needs is in people’s heads, not in a document.

If the organization already has a tool

When there is an existing tool and the organization wants to move to a new one, I start by understanding the current one before designing anything new:

  • What it does today. Its features, integrations and how people actually use it.
  • What it fails to do. The gaps that made the organization want to move.
  • What is inside it. The inventory of accounts, safes or folders, policies and owners it holds.

Then I run a discovery exercise, write the migration plan and the processes around it, and get sign-off from the stakeholders before anything is moved.

The plan also covers the end of the old tool. Its audit logs and session recordings often have to be kept for a retention period, so I plan how they will be archived, who can still read them, and when the old tool is finally switched off.

The order I follow

  1. Listen

    Talk to stakeholders across the business units about the organization, regions, risks and infrastructure. If there is an existing tool, assess it and plan the migration, as above.

  2. Prove

    Run a proof of concept (POC) with more than one tool, against the real problems found in step one.

  3. Decide and engage

    Look at the POC results and decide which one to buy. Once licenses and tools are bought, start the conversation with professional services: the statement of work (SOW), the engagement letter, the training program and support.

  4. Design and plan

    In the meantime, the architect prepares the PAM architecture: the master architecture, the high-level design (HLD) and the low-level design (LLD). The architecture is approved by the professional services team before implementation starts. Then comes the inventory analysis, and the inventory sign-off by interviewing stakeholders. On that basis we build the project plan, the wave plan and the remediation plan, and write the use cases, the business use cases, and the functional and non-functional requirements. The PAM roadmap and strategy are documented too.

  5. Build the foundations

    The policies the platform will enforce are reviewed and written down before they are built: the break-glass process, the password policy, the naming conventions for groups and roles, and the vaulting security. Each one is documented and approved by the business.

  6. Deliver in phases

    The project plan runs in stages. First a small build in Dev, as a limited POC. Then pre-prod. Finally production, and go-live. System integration testing (SIT) and user acceptance testing (UAT) sit inside these phases. Every move from one environment to the next has written go/no-go criteria and a rollback plan. Before go-live the platform itself gets a security review, including hardening and a penetration test, because it holds the keys to everything. Performance and load testing are part of the non-functional requirements.

  7. Train, go live and support

    End-user training comes last, and each business unit has a champion who helps their colleagues. After go-live there is a hypercare period, with a clear support model: who answers, how fast, and how a problem escalates. The success measures agreed at the start are reviewed once the platform has been running for a while.

Requirements: what it must do, and how well

I split the requirements in two, and I trace each one to a use case and a test, so nothing is promised that cannot be checked.

  • Functional. What the platform does: onboard accounts, vault and rotate credentials, broker and record sessions, run approvals, report, and expose an API for automation.
  • Non-functional. How well it does it: availability, performance, scalability, security, recoverability, supportability, usability and audit-readiness.

Access to the platform follows the joiner, mover and leaver process. Privileged roles are given through groups in the identity provider, named by the approved naming conventions. When someone changes role or leaves, their membership is removed, and the credentials they owned are reassigned to a new owner.

Availability and disaster recovery

A PAM platform cannot be the thing that is down when something goes wrong. Because it now runs in the cloud, I design it to stay up:

  • High availability. Redundant components spread across availability zones, so the loss of one does not stop the service. Upgrades and patches are planned so the platform stays online.
  • Disaster recovery (DR). A second region, with a recovery time objective (RTO) and a recovery point objective (RPO) agreed with the business, written into the design.
  • Backup and restore. Backups are taken, and restoring from them is tested. A backup that has never been restored is only a hope.
  • Test it. Failover and DR are exercised before go-live and on a schedule afterwards, not just described in a document.
  • Break-glass has to work when the rest does not. Emergency access is designed so it still works if the PAM platform or the identity provider is unavailable.

Sign-in: SSO and MFA

  • Single sign-on (SSO). People sign in to the PAM platform through the organization’s identity provider, for example Microsoft Entra ID, using a standard protocol such as SAML or OpenID Connect. The platform does not hold a second set of passwords for people. The few local accounts that remain are documented and monitored.
  • Multi-factor authentication (MFA). MFA is enforced at the identity provider. For privileged users the method matters: phishing-resistant options such as FIDO2 passkeys or security keys, not SMS or voice calls. Microsoft itself recommends phishing-resistant MFA for administrator roles.
  • Step-up for the riskiest actions. Where the product supports it, actions like checking out the most sensitive credential, or approving a high-risk request, ask for MFA again.
  • Who is covered. Employees, contractors and third-party users, each with an identity that can be switched off when their work ends.
  • What gets documented. The protocol settings, how identity provider groups map to PAM roles, session lengths, and what happens when the identity provider is down.

Conditional access

Conditional access decides, at the moment of sign-in, whether access is allowed and on what terms. In Microsoft Entra it looks at who the user is (user, group or role), which application they want, where they are coming from (IP address or country), the device, and the sign-in risk. It can then block, require MFA or a stronger authentication method, require a compliant device, or ask the user to sign in again more often.

For the PAM platform I typically design policies like these:

  • Privileged users must use a phishing-resistant authentication method.
  • Privileged access must come from a managed, compliant device.
  • Sign-ins from countries where the business has no presence are blocked.
  • Risky sign-ins are blocked, or asked for step-up.
  • Privileged sessions ask for re-authentication more often than ordinary work.

New policies start in report-only mode, so I can see who they would have affected, and only then are they enforced. Two exclusions need care. Emergency access (break-glass) accounts are excluded from policies that block or restrict sign-in, because otherwise they could be unusable in exactly the emergency they exist for. Service accounts and service principals are also left out of user policies, and are covered separately using controls for workload identities, or replaced with managed identities.

Other identity providers offer similar controls under different names. The design document records which policies apply and why.

Vaulting credentials

What goes in the vault. Human privileged accounts (domain admin, local admin, root, database admin), service accounts, application secrets, SSH keys, API tokens and keys, privileged cloud accounts, and network device accounts. For AI agents, the rule is no shared long-lived keys: each agent gets scoped, short-lived credentials and a named owner.

How it is organized. Credentials are grouped into safes or folders (the name depends on the product) by owner, application and tier, with membership limited to the people and systems that need it. They are named by the approved naming conventions.

  1. Discover and assign an owner

    Find the account, confirm what it is used for, and name who owns it.

  2. Onboard and verify

    Add it to the vault with the right policy, and check that the vault can actually log in with it.

  3. Rotate straight away

    Change the credential immediately, so any old, widely known value stops working.

  4. Plan recovery

    Decide how to bring the vault and the target back in step if they ever fall out of sync.

  5. Use under control

    Access needs a reason and, for sensitive accounts, an approval and a ticket. Where possible the session is brokered so the person never sees the password. Critical credentials can require two approvers and are changed after each use.

  6. Protect the vault itself

    Encryption at rest, with the encryption keys held in a key management service or a hardware security module (HSM). Vault administrators are separated from the people who use secrets. Backups are encrypted. Every access is logged and sent to the SIEM.

The vaulting security document is reviewed and approved by the business before any of this is built.

Integrations

The new PAM solution has to work with the tools people already use. I document each integration.

  • ServiceNow. Requests and approvals. The platform checks that a valid incident or change ticket exists, can link the session to it, and access can be removed when the ticket closes.
  • Microsoft Entra. SSO, MFA and conditional access, as above.
  • The security information and event management (SIEM) system. The platform sends sign-ins, approvals, credential check-outs, session starts and stops, rotations and their failures, configuration changes, and any use of break-glass. Alerts cover break-glass use, access without a ticket, after-hours access to the most sensitive systems, and failed rotations.

Break-glass, onboarded properly

The break-glass process is onboarded as part of the program and follows the organization’s own policy. It is not left as a side arrangement. It is tested with a drill before go-live and again on a schedule, so people know it works before they need it.

How the program is run

  • Weekly retrospectives. Professional services, third parties and the customer meet every week to say what worked and what did not.
  • Weekly updates to executives. A short progress report, so nothing is a surprise.
  • A risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies log. One place to track what could go wrong, reviewed in the weekly meeting.
  • Success measures. Agreed at the start, so there is a clear way to tell whether the program worked.
  • Change management. A communications plan, and a champion in each business unit.
  • An exception path. Accounts that cannot be onboarded go on a register with an owner, a reason and an end date.
  • Operations handover. The operations document is written and approved before the platform is handed to the customer.

Before I call it ready

A short check, so nothing is missed:

  • Every credential in scope has an owner, and the inventory has been signed off.
  • SSO and MFA are enforced, and conditional access is enforced, not just in report-only mode.
  • The vaulting standard, password policy, naming conventions and break-glass process are approved.
  • High availability and disaster recovery have been tested, and a restore has been tried.
  • Break-glass has been drilled, and the alert fires.
  • Logs reach the SIEM, and the alerts have an owner.
  • Go/no-go criteria were met, and a rollback plan exists.
  • Runbooks, the troubleshooting document and the operations document are written and approved.
  • People are trained, and the support model is in place.
  • The old tool has a decommission date, and its old logs are archived.
