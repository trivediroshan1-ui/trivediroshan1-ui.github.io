Just-in-Time & Just-Enough-Access Elevation

Replacing standing administrative access with time-boxed, scope-limited elevation that closes automatically.

Credential Management Platform Owner & PAM Product Owner · Last reviewed 1 Oct 2026

The short version

  • Admin rights granted as standing role membership outlive the task that justified them. Audits then depend on people remembering to take access back.
  • The fix is elevation with two limits: a short window and a narrow scope. Each request opens a window, and the window closes on its own.
  • I size the window from what people really did with the role. An advisory step can propose scope and duration, but the requester and the approver still decide.

The problem

Administrative rights were granted as standing role membership. Someone needed access for a migration, got added to a role, and stayed in it long after the migration ended. Every audit relied on people remembering to revoke what they no longer needed, and remembering is a weak control.

A privileged account that is always on is always worth stealing. MITRE ATT&CK lists Valid Accounts (T1078) as a technique that adversaries use for initial access, persistence, privilege escalation and defense evasion. A standing admin role hands them all four for as long as the account exists. Shrinking the hours in which an account is privileged shrinks what a stolen credential can do.

Two terms that get mixed up

Just-in-time (JIT) is about time. Privilege is activated when a task needs it and ends when the window ends.

JEA has a precise meaning at Microsoft: Just Enough Administration, a PowerShell feature. You write role capability files (.psrc) that list the cmdlets, functions and commands a role may run, and a session configuration (.pssc) that says who can connect, which roles they get and which identity the session runs as. Users connect to a constrained endpoint. The session can run as a virtual account, which Microsoft describes as a temporary local account that is destroyed when the session ends.

In this case study the title says “Just-Enough-Access”, which is the wider idea: give the smallest scope that does the job, on any platform. I write “JEA” only when I mean Microsoft’s feature, and I write “just-enough scope” for the general idea. The two work well together. JIT limits when, and just-enough scope limits what.

The mechanism

The diagram compares one engineer over one week. Standing access is a constant bar. Elevation is a set of short windows, each one requested, approved, used and closed.

The same idea in different places

Elevation looks different depending on what you are elevating into. These are the patterns I reach for, in the order I usually roll them out.

A vendor-neutral summary comes from the UK National Cyber Security Center. Instead of using a credential to reach an administration interface, the administrator uses it to request access, and if the request is approved a temporary credential is issued. The NCSC also says that shortening how long an administration credential can be used cuts the attacker’s window if one is stolen.

One elevation, start to finish

  1. Ask for a task, not a role

    The requester names the target, the task, the ticket or change number and how long they think they need. “Admin on everything for a week” is not a valid request. Microsoft’s PIM has a ticket field, but its documentation says it is information only and does not check the number against a ticketing system. So the service desk does the checking.

    GuardrailNo open ticket, no requestEvidenceRequest record

  2. Prove who they are, properly

    Sign-in goes through single sign-on, with multi-factor authentication and conditional access. The details matter here. PIM can require MFA on activation, and it can require a Conditional Access authentication context, for example a specific authentication strength or a compliant device. If someone has just reauthenticated for one activation, Microsoft says they are not asked again within ten minutes for other eligible roles.

    GuardrailNo MFA, no activationEvidenceSign-in log

  3. Get an advisory proposal

    A policy-aware assistant reads the request and proposes a scope and a duration, using what the role has really been used for. It is a suggestion. The requester can change it, and the approver sees it next to the request. The proposal can never go above the maximum the policy sets for that role.

    GuardrailProposal capped by policy, humans decideEvidenceProposal and final choice, both recorded

  4. Approve by policy, or by a person

    Roles with low risk and a pre-agreed use can be approved by policy. Anything touching production or the identity systems needs a named person. Approvals flow through the service desk (ServiceNow in my setup). PIM itself says approvers cannot approve their own activation requests, and that an unapproved request expires after 24 hours and has to be submitted again. I apply the same rule to every platform: the requester is never the approver.

    GuardrailSeparation of dutiesEvidenceApprover, time, reason

  5. Open the window

    The eligible assignment is activated, or the group membership is added with an expiry, or the broker session starts. The window carries its own end time from the first second. PIM’s activation maximum can be set between 1 and 24 hours per role.

    GuardrailSmallest role that does the task, end time set at the startEvidenceActivation record with start and end

  6. Work, and leave a trail

    Everything the person does is logged, and on brokered sessions recorded. Logs go to the SIEM so that the people who hold the privilege cannot edit the record of using it.

    GuardrailLogs stored outside the elevation toolEvidenceSession record or audit log tied to the ticket

  7. Close it and look back

    When the timer ends or the task is marked complete, the access is removed by the system. A follow-up ticket is never needed. Afterwards I look at windows that closed with no privileged action in them, because they tell me the role or the window was too big.

    GuardrailExpiry enforced by the system, not by memoryEvidenceExpiry event and the review of unused windows

When the work runs past the window

Change windows overrun. I handle this as a fresh request with fresh approval, never as a silent extension. The requester says why more time is needed, and the approver can see how long the first window ran. Microsoft’s documented extend and renew features apply to role assignments that are about to expire. When a user asks for one, an administrator has to approve it. For an activation that is running out, the simple rule is to request again.

When the elevation path is down

The identity provider, the service desk or the broker can be unavailable at the worst moment. Break glass stays available for exactly this. Microsoft’s guidance for Entra is two or more cloud-only emergency accounts, made permanently active and not eligible, with phishing-resistant sign-in, alerts on every use, and a check at least every 90 days. The NCSC says to protect the break-glass process carefully. I cover the design in Break-Glass Emergency Access Design.

Accounts that cannot be elevated just in time

A scheduled task or a service account has no person to request anything at 3 a.m. Forcing it through this flow only produces a service account with permanent approval. These identities go to the credential platform instead, where they get an owner, a rotation policy or a short-lived credential. The two case studies fit together: Credential Management Platform Strategy for the machine side and Non-Human Identity at Scale for the scale problem.

Example: fixing a network rule in one subscription

This is an illustration, not a specific event. The times are made up to show the shape.

Before

  • The engineer has held a broad role on the whole subscription since a project two years ago.
  • The role was never reviewed, because removing it would mean a ticket nobody owns.
  • A stolen session would work at any hour, on everything in the subscription.

After

  • The engineer is eligible for a narrower role on one resource group.
  • They ask for 90 minutes, citing the change ticket. The assistant suggests 90 minutes and the network role, and the approver accepts.
  • MFA and conditional access run at activation, and the window closes at the 90-minute mark.
  • The activation, the approver and the reason are all in the log.

For the wider picture of zero standing privilege, brokers and session recording, see PAM Modernization & Zero Standing Privilege. This page stays with the elevation step itself.

How to get there without stalling

  1. 1. Map roles to tasksList every standing privileged role and the tasks it was really used for. This sizes the smallest workable window for each one, instead of one generic default.Done whenEvery standing role has a task list, a proposed scope, a window and an owner.
  2. 2. Start where the platform helpsMove cloud and directory roles to eligible assignments. The features for activation, approval and expiry already exist there.Done whenNo cloud or directory admin role is permanently active, apart from emergency accounts.
  3. 3. Wire approvals and evidenceConnect the service desk, conditional access and the SIEM, so that one request leaves one clear trail.Done whenAn auditor can follow any elevation from request to expiry without asking anyone.
  4. 4. Add the advisory stepStart in suggestion-only mode. Compare what it proposes with what people and approvers finally choose.Done whenApprovers trust the proposals enough to use them, and the policy cap has held every time.
  5. 5. Extend to servers, databases and KubernetesUse a broker, temporary accounts or JEA endpoints where they fit. Test break glass before you remove standing access.Done whenThe fallback has been exercised and the standing assignments have been removed.
  6. 6. Close the old pathKeep a register of exceptions with owners and end dates. Review it on a rhythm.Done whenStanding privileged access is the exception, and each exception has an end date.

On window length, Microsoft’s PIM deployment plan gives examples. One hour for Global Administrator, two hours for Exchange Administrator and eight hours for Helpdesk Administrator. They are examples of how different roles deserve different windows, and I treat them as a starting point to check against what people really do.

What I measure

  • Time to access. The time from request to an open window, including approval. If this is slow, people will fight the process. I look at the slow tail as much as the median.
  • Share of privileged access that is standing. Permanent active assignments as a share of all privileged assignments, by role and by platform. The goal is a number that keeps falling, with every exception on the register.
  • Expired without use. Windows that closed with no privileged action. A high share means the role is too broad, the window is too long, or people are asking “just in case”.
  • Extensions and re-requests. How often a window had to be requested again, by role. A high count suggests the default window is too short for that task.
  • Approvals that timed out. Requests that went unanswered. They show approvers who are overloaded or unclear about ownership.

A practical point on retention: Microsoft says PIM audit data is available for the past 30 days, and suggests routing it to Azure Monitor with diagnostic settings if you need it for longer. I send it to the SIEM from day one so the audit period never depends on the tool’s default.

  • Changes to windows and scopes go through change control. A longer default window is a risk decision, and it has a named owner.
  • The advisory step is governed like any other policy input. Its rules, caps and changes are documented and approved, and its proposals are logged beside the final decision.
  • Exceptions have owners and end dates. Anything left as standing access sits on a register and is reviewed on a rhythm.

What tends to go wrong

  • Approval fatigue. If approvers see hundreds of requests a week, they start approving without reading. Pre-agreed roles can go through policy, so people only look at the risky ones.
  • Windows set wrong in both directions. Too short and people get cut off in the middle of a change. Too long and the window is standing access with a new name.
  • Eligible becomes the new standing. If everyone is eligible for everything, the list of eligible people is the list of targets. Eligible accounts still need strong sign-in and regular review.
  • The elevation path becomes a dependency. When the identity provider or service desk is down, nobody can fix anything. This is why the fallback has to be built and tested first.
  • JEA endpoints with loose roles. Microsoft warns against wildcards in role capabilities and against allowing commands such as Start-Process, and notes that a local administrator can change a JEA configuration. A JEA endpoint is only as tight as its role file.
  • Legacy systems with no concept of expiry. Some platforms cannot remove access on a timer. A broker session or a scripted removal can close the gap, and the rest goes on the exceptions register.
  • Adoption friction. Engineers who used to have instant access now wait. If the wait is long or the form is long, they will look for ways around it.

What I have learned to do differently

These are habits I keep, not a checklist from any one project.

  • I map the role to its tasks before I pick a number. A default window copied from somewhere else is a guess. A window sized from what people really did with the role is something I can defend.
  • I keep the form short. Target, task, ticket, length. Every extra field is a reason to look for a shortcut.
  • I start where the platform already does the work. Cloud and directory roles first, because activation, approval and expiry exist there. Those early results buy trust for the harder platforms.
  • I keep the advisory step advisory. It proposes, people decide, and I log both. I also look at where people disagree with it, because that is where the policy needs work.
  • I test the fallback before I remove standing access. Not after. The day you take the last standing role away is the wrong day to learn that break glass does not work.
  • I sit with approvers. An approver who cannot see the ticket, the target and the requested length cannot make a good decision, so the request has to carry that context.
  • I publish time to access. Engineers accept a new control more easily when they can see that somebody is watching how long it takes.
  • I keep service accounts out of this flow from day one. They have a different answer, and mixing the two slows both.
