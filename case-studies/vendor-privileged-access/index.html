Third-Party & Vendor Privileged Access Governance

Giving vendors, consultants and managed service providers privileged access with the same scrutiny as internal privileged accounts, not less.

Credential Management Platform Owner & PAM Product Owner · Last reviewed 1 Oct 2026

The short version

  • Vendor access goes wrong when it takes the fastest path: a shared login, a standing VPN account, an exception nobody comes back to. The fix is to treat a vendor engineer like any other privileged user, plus a few extra rules about who sponsors them and when it ends.
  • Each vendor person gets their own named account, a named internal sponsor, MFA and an end date. They reach the target through a broker that records the session. They never get a network path of their own.
  • Expiry does the offboarding. If nothing renews the access, it ends, and the sponsor has to say yes again to keep it.

The problem

Most companies have rules for how an employee gets privileged access. The rules assume an employee: someone in the HR system, with a manager, a start date and a leaving date. A support engineer from a software supplier, a consultant on a six-week project or a managed service provider’s night team fits none of that. So when one of them needs to get in, the quickest route wins. That is usually a shared account the vendor already uses, a VPN login that was set up for the last project and never closed, or an exception somebody signed off on a Friday afternoon.

Each of those shortcuts removes something an internal account would have had. A shared account removes the name, so you cannot say who did what. A standing VPN login removes the end date, and with it any natural moment to ask whether the access is still needed. An ad hoc exception removes the review, because nobody owns it. Put together, the people with the least accountability to you end up with some of the widest and longest-lived access in the company.

There is a second gap that is easy to miss. Your HR system tells you when an employee leaves. Nothing tells you when a vendor’s engineer leaves their employer. The contract can end, the person can move on, and the account stays exactly as it was. This page describes how I set up third-party privileged access so that it is held to the same standard as internal privileged accounts. The rest of the program it sits in is described in How I Run a Privileged Access Program.

Before anyone gets in: contract and onboarding

The technical controls only work if the paperwork backs them. I ask the contract owner for a short list of clauses before the first request is raised, and I would rather have them in a new vendor’s contract than chase them later.

  • Individual accounts only. The vendor agrees that every person uses their own named account and never shares or passes on a credential.
  • Notice when someone leaves. The vendor tells the vendor manager when a person with access leaves the engagement or the company, within a stated time.
  • Sessions may be recorded. The vendor accepts that privileged sessions are recorded and the recordings are kept, and that its staff have been told.
  • Named subcontractors. Any subcontractor who might use the access is disclosed first. They go through the same onboarding as everyone else.
  • Security requirements flow down. The vendor meets the company’s security requirements for its own laptops and accounts, and reports an incident that could affect us.
  • Access ends with the contract. Everything tied to the contract is switched off when it ends or is terminated, and the vendor does not keep copies of anything it was given.

On the identity side, I keep vendor people in a distinct account type. That lets the directory enforce rules that would be wrong for staff: a maximum lifetime, no self-service extensions, a mandatory sponsor field and a stricter MFA policy. It also turns the question “how many external people can get into production today?” into a query instead of an investigation.

How a vendor session travels

The diagram follows one vendor engineer from onboarding to the target system. The two controls that carry the most weight are the sponsor, who owns the decision, and the broker, which owns the path. Everything else supports one of those two.

The broker is what removes the standing network path. A VPN gives a vendor a place on your network and trusts them to behave once inside. A broker gives them one session to one target, which the company opens and closes. Because the broker also records, the same component answers the audit question about what actually happened.

The workflow, one step at a time

Seven steps, each with the guardrail that holds it in place and the evidence it leaves behind.

  1. Onboard the vendor, then the person

    Before any request, the vendor company is registered against a contract and a named vendor manager on our side. Each engineer is then created as an individual, with their own account in the company directory, marked as an external person, with the vendor’s name and the contract reference on it. The vendor’s account lead confirms in writing that this person works for them and has accepted the usage rules. Nobody gets a shared login, including the team that “only ever needs one”.

    GuardrailOne person, one account. Shared vendor logins are not created.EvidenceOnboarding record, signed usage rules, contract reference

  2. Request, with a reason and an end date

    An engineer cannot ask for “access to the environment”. The request names one system or a short list, the role they need on it, why, and when it stops. The form will not accept a blank end date, and it will not accept one past the end of the contract. The internal sponsor is named on the request and has to be an employee.

    GuardrailRequest is tied to a contract and capped at its end dateEvidenceRequest ticket

  3. The sponsor approves, with the scope in plain words

    The sponsor is the person who asked the vendor to do the work. Next to the technical scope string, an advisory summary says in plain language what the grant means, for example “can run any command as the database administrator on these two production servers for five days”. It is advice and nothing more. The sponsor sees the real scope too and decides for themselves, and the text they were shown is stored with the approval. A sponsor cannot approve their own access, and security can be added as a second approver for the most sensitive systems.

    GuardrailNamed employee approves; the summary never approves anythingEvidenceApproval record with the summary text shown

  4. Connect through the broker, after MFA

    The engineer signs in to the company directory with MFA, and conditional access can ask for a managed device or a known location. They then open a session in the broker, which is the only thing that can reach the target. The vendor has no VPN account and no inbound route to the network. Where the tooling allows it, the broker supplies the target credential itself so the engineer never sees it.

    GuardrailMFA on every sign-in; no direct network pathEvidenceSign-in log, broker connection log

  5. Monitor the session

    The broker records what happens, so the session can be replayed. Commands that match a short high-risk list raise an alert in the SIEM. The sponsor or the security team can watch a live session and end it. The recording and the logs are held somewhere the broker’s own administrators cannot edit.

    GuardrailRecording is on by default and the vendor cannot switch it offEvidenceSession recording, SIEM events

  6. Expire without anyone remembering

    At the end date the access stops. The account is disabled, any open session is closed, and nothing needs a ticket to make that happen. If the work runs long, the sponsor extends it for a stated period, which is a new approval with a new record.

    GuardrailAccess ends on its own; extension is a fresh decisionEvidenceExpiry event in the log

  7. Review on a set rhythm

    On a regular schedule the sponsor sees every vendor person with access and confirms or removes each one. The vendor manager checks the same list against the vendor’s current staff. A leaver notice from the vendor removes the person the same day, ahead of any review. Accounts that have not signed in for a set number of days are disabled and put in front of the sponsor.

    GuardrailSponsor confirms each person; idle accounts are disabledEvidenceCompleted review, with removals recorded

Example: a database vendor and a slow production query

This is an illustration, not a specific event.

Before

  • The vendor’s support team shares one login that was created for an earlier project.
  • It reaches the database server over a standing VPN account.
  • Nobody can say which engineer ran what, or whether the person who had the password last year still works there.
  • The access has no end date and no owner.

After

  • The engineer has a named account and a sponsor from the application team.
  • The request is for one database server, a read-only diagnostic role, for three days.
  • The sponsor approves after reading the plain-language summary, then the engineer signs in with MFA and works through the broker.
  • The session is recorded. On day three the access closes, and a further day needs a new approval.

When a vendor is needed at 2 a.m.

Outages do not wait for an approval queue, and a vendor is sometimes the only one who can fix the product. If there is no fast lane, people build one: a password sent over chat, or an account left switched on for next time. I would rather design the fast lane on purpose.

The fast lane uses the same path. The on-call internal engineer acts as sponsor, the window is measured in hours, and someone on our side watches the session live. The review happens the next working day: the sponsor writes down why, and security looks at the recording. There are no standing emergency vendor accounts. Whatever is created for the incident is removed automatically when the window closes. The longer treatment of urgent access is in Break-Glass Access.

When a vendor person leaves

There are four ways this should be caught, and I do not rely on any one of them. The vendor tells us, as the contract requires. The end date arrives. The sponsor drops the person at review. Or the account has been idle long enough that it is disabled and queried. The first is the fastest and the least reliable, because it depends on someone at another company remembering to send an email. That is why the end date and the idle check exist.

The contract end is the larger cut. Every account is tied to a contract reference, so closing the contract closes everything attached to it in one step, and the report afterwards should show no active external account against that reference.

How to build it without stalling

  1. 1. Find out who has access todayList every vendor with any access: shared accounts, VPN logins, remote support tools, cloud guest users. Ask the vendor managers, not just IT.Done whenThere is one list of vendors and the people and accounts that can reach production.
  2. 2. Write the contract and onboarding standardAgree the clause list, the external account type, the sponsor rule and the one-page sponsor guide.Done whenA new vendor person can be onboarded without anyone improvising.
  3. 3. Put the broker in front of the riskiest targetsStart with production databases, domain controllers and cloud consoles, with MFA and recording on.Done whenVendor access to those targets is only possible through the broker.
  4. 4. Automate expiry and reviewEnd dates enforced by the directory, idle accounts disabled, review tasks sent to sponsors.Done whenAccess ends and reviews arrive without a person remembering.
  5. 5. Close the old pathsDisable the shared accounts and standing VPN logins one vendor at a time, with the sponsor.Done whenNew vendor access cannot be created outside this path, and the old list is empty.

Governance: who owns what

RoleOwns
Internal sponsorWhether the work needs this access. Approves each request and each renewal, and confirms every person at review. Must be an employee.
Vendor managerThe relationship day to day. Keeps the vendor’s staff list current, receives leaver notices, and confirms at review that each person still works for the vendor.
SecurityThe access standard, the external account rules, the high-risk command list and exception criteria. Watches recordings and alerts, and acts as second approver on the most sensitive targets.
Contract ownerThe clauses: individual accounts, leaver notice, recording, subcontractors, flow-down and end of contract. Keeps the contract reference and end date accurate, because the access end date follows it.
Platform teamRuns the directory integration and the broker, and keeps the log store separate from its own administrators.
  • The sponsor is the hard role to fill. A sponsor who clicks approve without reading is the main way this fails, so I write their job on one page and keep it short.
  • Exceptions have owners and end dates. Anything outside the path sits on a register, reviewed on a rhythm, and removed when its reason ends.

What tends to go wrong

  • Sponsors approve without reading. The plain-language summary helps, but it is advisory and it can be wrong, which is why the real scope sits next to it and the approval keeps both.
  • Vendor turnover is invisible. Vendor staff change often and nobody tells you. The end date, the idle check and the vendor manager’s confirmation each catch some of it, and none catches all of it.
  • The vendor wants its own tool. Many vendors have a favourite remote support product. Letting it in recreates the direct path. Say no early and offer the broker instead.
  • Named accounts do not fit every system. Some older systems only support a shared admin login. Treat each as a named exception with a sponsor, a plan and an end date, and put the broker in front of it so the session is still attributable.
  • Short expiry creates pressure. If extending is painful, people ask for long end dates up front. Make renewal quick but explicit, and the short default will hold.
  • The fast lane becomes the main lane. If emergency access is easier than the normal path, it will be used for everything. Track how often it is used and look at each use.
  • Recordings pile up unwatched. A recording nobody reads does little. Sample a few with the sponsor, and alert on a short list of commands that matter.
  • Old contracts have no clauses. Vendors signed before the standard existed will not have them. Add them at renewal and compensate with controls in the meantime.

What I have learned to do differently

These are habits I keep when I run this kind of work. They are not a record of one project.

  • I start with the list of who has access today. Before designing anything I ask the vendor managers and the application owners who the vendors are and how they get in. The list is nearly always longer than the one IT holds, and it makes the case better than any slide.
  • I put the sponsor in the room early. Sponsors are application and business owners, not security people. I walk through the request screen with two or three of them and change the wording until they can say what they are approving.
  • I make renewing easy and ending hard to forget. If extending takes five minutes, nobody needs a six-month end date. The default stays short because the exception is cheap.
  • I test with one friendly vendor first. A vendor who will tell me where the broker gets in their way is worth more than a pilot with an internal team that does not use it the way a vendor does.
  • I read a few recordings myself. Reviewing real sessions with the sponsor shows what the vendor does that nobody thought to ask about, and whether the alert list is any good.
  • I plan for the broker being down. Once vendors depend on it, an outage blocks real work. There is a tested, approved fallback that goes through the same sponsor and logging, not a quiet exception.
  • I close the old path with the vendor, not on them. Switching off a standing account without warning damages the relationship and teaches people to hide the next one. We agree a date, and I confirm the new path works first.

The same habits appear in PAM Modernization & Zero Standing Privilege, where the aim is also to remove access that nobody is using.
