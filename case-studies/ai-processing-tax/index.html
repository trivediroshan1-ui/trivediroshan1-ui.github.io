AI Governance · Decision Architecture

AI made producing work free. It didn’t make deciding on it free.

A real reaction to a real conversation, checked against four independent studies, and answered with a structural control instead of a slogan.

Independent Research & Control Design · Last reviewed 1 Oct 2026

The short version

  • AI collapsed the cost of producing a document, a report or an access request to near zero. It did not collapse the cost of verifying, judging or deciding on that output, and that cost does not disappear. It moves to whoever opens the file next.
  • That cost tends to land on the people with the least slack to absorb it. I call it the processing tax.
  • The fix I specified is structural. A decision-package gate sits where an AI agent hands a request to a human and sends back any handoff that has not done its own thinking first. It checks completeness, never correctness, so the human keeps the judgment.

The problem

This did not start as a thesis. It started as a reaction to a real conversation about AI and workplace productivity, and I wrote it down before I reached for any framework:

“AI is creating situations challenging rather than making it easier when it comes to take a decision… it’s not landing properly.”
Roshan, reacting in the thread

What I was reacting to is a mismatch in costs. Asking an AI tool for a document, a summary, a status report or an access request takes seconds. Reading that output, checking it, working out what it is really asking for and deciding what to do about it takes about as long as it always did. Sometimes it takes longer, because the output looks finished and the reader has to work out what is missing.

Work has two halves: producing the thing and deciding on the thing. Only the first half got cheap. The second half is still done by a person, and a person has a fixed number of hours. When one side of a process speeds up and the other does not, the queue forms in front of the slow side.

The cost also moves in a particular direction. The person who produced the output saved time. The person who receives it pays for that saving. Receivers tend to be the people who decide: approvers, managers, reviewers, owners. These are the people with the least room in their day, because deciding is their job on top of everything else. So the cost concentrates upward, toward the people least able to absorb it.

In identity and access work this is easy to picture. An AI agent can raise a hundred access requests before lunch. Each one is cheap to send. Each one asks a human for a yes or a no. If the request arrives as a bare ask with no analysis attached, the approver has to rebuild the thinking from scratch every time, and approval becomes a chore the approver learns to get through quickly.

What the research says, and what it does not

I did not want to build a control on one conversation, so I checked the reaction against four independent sources. They measure different things, and none of them measures the processing tax directly. What they have in common is that the gains people expect from AI are harder to find than the speed of producing output would suggest.

  • BetterUp Labs and the Stanford Social Media Lab coined the word “workslop” in a Harvard Business Review article in September 2025. BetterUp’s own write-up defines it as AI-generated content that looks polished and complete but is unhelpful, low-quality or off the mark. The article’s point that matters here is where the effort goes: onto the colleagues who have to revise or replace it.
  • MIT Media Lab’s Project NANDA published “The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025” in July 2025. It looks at why enterprise AI projects stall, and a core reason it gives is that most systems do not retain feedback, adapt to context or improve over time. I read this through a trade-press summary, not the report itself.
  • NBER executive surveys. Two NBER working papers survey senior executives about AI and productivity, one of nearly 6,000 executives across four countries and one of about 750 corporate executives. The second reports a disconnect between the productivity gains executives perceive and the gains that are measured.
  • Goldman Sachs Research, in March 2026, said it still found no meaningful relationship between productivity and AI adoption at the economy-wide level, while finding gains in specific use cases such as customer support and software development. I read this through a news report on the note, not the note itself.

I would not stretch these into proof. Some are surveys of opinion, some are trade-press summaries of a report, and none looks at approval queues. My claim is narrower. Four unrelated measurements point the same way: producing output got cheap, and turning that output into a result did not.

Why a human reviewer is not enough on their own

Putting a person at the end of the line does not fix the problem, because people who check automated recommendations can drift into agreeing with them. Researchers call this automation bias. In a well-known 1999 study of a simulated flight task, Skitka and colleagues found that people using a slightly unreliable automated aid made two kinds of error: they missed events the system did not flag, and they followed its recommendations even when other valid indicators contradicted them. A 2012 systematic review by Goddard, Roudsari and Wyatt looked at how often this happens, what influences it and what reduces it.

Regulators have noticed too. Article 14 of the EU AI Act, on human oversight of high-risk AI systems, says the people assigned to oversight should remain aware of the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output of the system. I read this as support for a design point. If a human is the safeguard, the way the work reaches them decides how well they can do it. A bare yes-or-no ask invites a quick yes. A package that sets out options, a recommendation and a confidence level gives the person something to disagree with.

How it fits together

The diagram shows the same handoff two ways. In the top lane, an AI agent sends a raw request straight to a human approver, who has to verify it, rebuild the context and decide. All the effort lands on one person. In the bottom lane, the agent first builds a decision package. A gate checks that the package is complete. A complete package reaches the human as a quick judgment call, and an incomplete one goes back to the agent with the gap named.

The gate checks completeness, not correctness. It cannot tell whether the agent’s recommendation is right. It can tell whether the agent did its own thinking, and that is the part a rule can check reliably. The right-or-wrong call stays with the human.

One handoff, start to finish

Steps 3 and 4 are the control itself. Before a request reaches a human, the originating agent must supply the options it considered, its recommendation, a confidence level and the one question that needs human judgment. The other steps are how I would wire that control into a working process. Each step has a guardrail and the evidence it leaves behind.

  1. Decide that a human is needed

    The agent only hands off the decisions that policy says need a person. Everything else it handles inside its own limits. A handoff is a cost, so the list of things that qualify should be short and written down.

    GuardrailA catalog of decisions that need a humanEvidenceReason code on every handoff

  2. Do the analysis first

    The agent works out what it is asking before it asks. It lists the options it considered, including the option of doing nothing, and picks one. This is the work the approver would otherwise do.

    GuardrailNo handoff without a recommendationEvidenceOptions considered, as recorded

  3. Build the decision package

    Four required fields: the options considered, the recommendation, a confidence level, and the one question that actually needs human judgment. The last field forces the agent to narrow a broad ask down to the part only a person can answer.

    GuardrailFixed package format with required fieldsEvidenceThe package as submitted

  4. Check it for completeness

    The gate looks for the required fields. It does not judge whether the recommendation is good. A package missing a field goes back to the originating agent automatically, with the specific gap named, before a human sees it.

    GuardrailIncomplete package is rejected, not queuedEvidenceRejection record with the missing field

  5. Put it in front of a person with a clock

    The approver sees the package, the risk score and a live countdown to expiry. The approval is time-boxed, so a stale request lapses instead of waiting to be approved by habit. The gate sits next to these existing controls as a fourth check: has this request earned a human’s attention.

    GuardrailTime-boxed approval with risk scoringEvidenceApproval timestamps and the risk score shown

  6. Let the human disagree, and say why

    The person can accept, change or refuse the recommendation. A refusal costs them nothing and needs only a short reason. If saying no is harder than saying yes, the package turns into a rubber stamp.

    GuardrailReject and modify are first-class optionsEvidenceDecision and reason, recorded

  7. Check the recommendations afterwards

    Because the gate only checks completeness, correctness has to be checked somewhere else. A periodic look at a sample of past packages, comparing what the agent recommended with what the human decided and what happened next, shows whether the recommendations deserve the trust they get.

    GuardrailRegular sampled review of past handoffsEvidenceReview notes and agreement rate with human decisions

A worked example

This is an illustration of the idea, not a description of a real event or a real system.

Say an AI agent that supports a delivery pipeline needs write access to a data store so a release can finish. It has to ask a person.

Without the gate

  • The approver receives one line: please approve write access for the pipeline account.
  • They have to find out which store, why, for how long and what the alternatives are.
  • They either spend a chunk of their day investigating or they approve to clear the queue.
  • Nobody can later see what was weighed.

With the gate

  • Options: read-only access, time-boxed write access, or decline and finish the release by hand.
  • Recommendation: time-boxed write access, ending when the release does.
  • Confidence: medium, because the agent could not confirm which tables the release touches.
  • The one question: is the release window a good enough reason to allow write access outside the usual process?

In the second case the approver reads a short package and answers a single question. They can still say no, and they can still notice that the confidence is only medium and ask for the table list first. If the agent had left out the confidence level, the gate would have sent the request back before the approver saw it.

How to build it without stalling

  1. 1. Map the handoffsList where AI agents and tools pass work to people today: access requests, approvals, reviews, exceptions. Note who receives each one.Done whenEvery handoff has a named receiver and a rough sense of how often it happens.
  2. 2. Define the packageAgree the required fields with the people who receive the requests. Ask them what they wish every request already contained.Done whenA receiver can read a sample package and say it answers their usual questions.
  3. 3. Run the gate in watch modeCheck packages and report what would have been rejected, without blocking anything yet.Done whenThe reasons for rejection are understood and the agents can fix them.
  4. 4. Turn on enforcementStart with the handoffs that reach the busiest approvers and the highest-risk decisions.Done whenAn incomplete handoff cannot reach a human in the covered flows.
  5. 5. Review and tuneSample past packages, collect feedback from approvers, and adjust the fields and the list of decisions that need a human.Done whenThe review runs on a fixed rhythm and someone owns the changes that come out of it.

A caution on the gate itself. A package can be complete and still wrong, and a confident, well-formatted wrong answer is more persuasive than a messy one. The gate keeps the work off the approver. It does not replace the approver’s judgment.

Governance: who owns what

Internal auditIndependent checking, separate from those who run the control
  • A rule that is enforced beats a culture that is hoped for. A norm such as “think before you send” only works while everyone remembers it. The gate holds the line in the exact place the handoff happens.
  • Ask the receivers. The people who approve are the ones who know what a good package looks like. They should help set the required fields and review them on a rhythm.
  • Watch the receiver’s load. Useful signals: how many handoffs reach each approver, how many are rejected at the gate, how often approvers refuse or change a recommendation, and how often a request expires unanswered. Pick the measures, then look at the trend, not a single day.
  • AI advises, people decide. The gate exists to protect that sentence in practice. The agent recommends and a person confirms, with the evidence in front of them.

What tends to go wrong

  • Agents learn to fill the fields. A complete package can be padded to pass the check. Options that are not real options, confidence that is always high. Sampled review is the answer to that, and it needs a human to read the packages.
  • The package becomes more reading. If the required fields are long, the gate has moved the tax and not reduced it. Keep the package short enough that a busy approver reads all of it.
  • Over-reliance continues. A well-made package makes agreement easy. The research on automation bias suggests this is a risk, so the refusal path, the time-box and the sampled review all matter.
  • The list of human decisions grows. Every team has a reason to add one more. Without an owner trimming the list, the number of handoffs creeps back up.
  • Rejections frustrate the agent owners. Early on, many packages will bounce. Watch mode before enforcement gives owners time to fix the common gaps.
  • Review fatigue is real in practice and hard to measure. I have not found a source on it that I could verify, so I do not cite one. I treat it as something to watch in the load measures above.

What I have learned to do differently

These are habits I keep, not a checklist from any one project.

  • Start from a real reaction. I begin with something someone said or something I felt when I used a tool, then check it against evidence. If I cannot find evidence, I say so and keep the idea small.
  • Separate what I have read from what I have measured. A survey of executives and a measured outcome are different things. I say which one I am quoting, and I say when I read a summary and not the original.
  • Ask who pays. When someone says a tool saves time, I ask who gets the time and who gets the extra work. The answer often shows up two steps later in the process.
  • Prefer a control to a reminder. If I want a behaviour, I try to build the check into the place where it happens. A reminder fades.
  • Check completeness in the system, and judgment with people. I let the machine do what a rule can verify and keep the call with a person who can say no.
  • Make saying no cheap. If refusing needs a long explanation, people stop refusing. I look at that first when a review process starts to feel like a formality.
  • Review the recommender, not just the decision. A good decision process includes a look back at whether the advice was any good.

My broader view of how AI and people should work together is on How I Use AI, and where I stop. The same principle runs through the other case studies: the model recommends and a person confirms.
