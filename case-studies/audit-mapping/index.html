IAM Audit & Control-Mapping Framework

How to connect what an IAM control is meant to do with the proof that it did, who holds that proof, and how often someone looks at it.

IAM control evaluation contributor · Last reviewed 1 Oct 2026

The short version

  • Audit wording and platform settings describe the same risk in different languages. The gap between them is where evidence goes missing and ownership gets vague.
  • Close it with a control register: one row per control, with a plain objective, one owner, the system setting that enforces it, the evidence it produces, and how fresh that evidence must be.
  • Collect evidence by a scheduled job into a store the audited people cannot edit. Record every gap with an owner and an expiry date. Then one control can answer to several frameworks without being written twice.

The problem

An auditor reads a requirement such as “access is reviewed periodically”. An engineer reads a screen of group settings. A control owner reads a policy paragraph. All three are talking about the same risk, and each of them is sure the other two have the proof.

The trouble shows up when the question arrives. Someone asks to see the last review of privileged access, and the answer is a search through email, a screenshot taken for a different purpose, and a person who has since moved teams. The control may well have been working. Nobody can show it, and the weeks spent reconstructing proof are weeks not spent improving anything.

The work I contributed to was evaluating IAM control intent against what the platforms could actually do and what the operating process actually did. The aim was a plain path from each design decision to the evidence that shows the control works, with a named owner at each point and a review that someone really performs.

From objective to auditor’s question

Every control gets the same chain. The objective says what the rule is for. The control says what the organization does about it. The system configuration is where that action is switched on. The configuration produces records, and a job gathers those records into a store. The auditor’s question is answered from that store, and what the auditor finds feeds back into the control. The register sits across the top and holds the owners, the frequency and any exceptions.

The register is the part people skip, so it is worth saying what a row holds.

  • Control ID and objective. One sentence in plain words, written so a non-specialist could check it.
  • Control statement. What is done, by whom, how often.
  • Control owner. One named role accountable for the control working.
  • System and configuration. The setting, policy or workflow where the control lives.
  • Evidence type, source and owner. What proves it, where it comes from, and who collects it.
  • Freshness. How recent the evidence has to be to count.
  • Framework tags. The requirements this one control is read against.
  • Gaps and exceptions. Each with an owner, a reason, a compensating step and an expiry date.

Four kinds of evidence, and what each one cannot show

Auditors collect evidence by examining records, interviewing people and testing the system. NIST’s assessment guidance names those three methods. An interview helps explain a control, but it is the weakest thing to rely on, so I design for the first and third. That means records the system produced and results anyone can re-run.

Evidence typeWhat it showsWhere it is weakFreshness it needs
Configuration exportHow a setting stands at one moment, such as a policy, a group list or a rotation ruleSays nothing about the weeks before or after. A change last month is invisible.Taken on the collection date, and again after every change to that setting
Log queryWhat happened across a period: sign-ins, approvals, privileged sessions, changesA wrong query or a missing log source looks the same as a clean resultCovers the whole audit period. The query text is saved with the result.
Ticket or approval recordA person asked, another person approved, the work was doneProves the process ran for that item, not that every item went through itPer item. Pair it with a list of all changes so the tickets can be matched to the whole population.
Review recordA named person looked at a list and decided to keep or remove each entryRubber-stamping. A signature without the list and the decisions proves very little.Dated inside the review cycle, with the list as it stood on the day

Most controls need two types together. A configuration export shows the rule. A log query or review record shows the rule was followed over time.

Freshness and sampling

Evidence goes stale. A screenshot from last year proves what a setting looked like last year. Each register row carries a freshness rule, and the collection job is built around it. Frequency also has to follow the strictest framework the control answers to. CIS Controls v8, for example, asks that active accounts are validated as authorized at a minimum of quarterly, and PCI DSS v4.0 asks for user accounts and privileges to be reviewed at least once every six months. A quarterly review satisfies both.

Sampling is the auditor’s call, and how many items they pick is theirs to decide. What I can control is the population they pick from and how the picks are recorded.

  • The population is complete. A full export of accounts, tickets or changes for the period, with the date and query that produced it. A sample from an incomplete list proves nothing about the rest.
  • The pick is recorded. How items were chosen, by whom, and when. If the auditor chooses, their selection is saved as it was given.

Mapping one control, step by step

  1. Write the objective in plain words

    Take the requirement as the framework words it, then restate it as one sentence about the risk. If the owner cannot read the sentence and say whether it is true, rewrite it.

    GuardrailNo jargon the owner cannot checkEvidenceRegister row

  2. Write the control and name one owner

    State what is done, by whom and how often. One role is accountable. A committee is not an owner, and neither is a team mailbox.

    GuardrailA row with no named owner does not go liveEvidenceOwner field and approval of the row

  3. Point to the configuration

    Name the system and the exact setting, policy or workflow that makes the control real. If the control depends on a person remembering, mark it as a manual control and plan its evidence accordingly.

    GuardrailManual controls are labeled as manualEvidenceConfiguration export

  4. Choose the evidence and its freshness

    Pick the evidence type from the four above, name the system it comes from, and set how recent it must be. Name a second evidence type if one alone would leave a gap, as with a setting that is shown but not shown to have been followed.

    GuardrailEvery row names its source and freshnessEvidenceEvidence plan in the register

  5. Collect by job, into a store people cannot edit

    A scheduled job pulls the evidence and writes it to the store with the date, the source and the query. Control owners and platform admins can read it. They cannot change it.

    GuardrailCollection is automated and the store is read-only to the auditedEvidenceCollection log and store permissions

  6. Review it, and sign the review

    A reviewer who is not the collector looks at what arrived. They check that it is complete, current and answers the objective. Their name and the date go on the record.

    GuardrailThe reviewer is not the person who collectedEvidenceSigned review record

  7. Record gaps and exceptions with an expiry

    Where the control or its evidence falls short, write it down. Each entry gets an owner, a reason, a compensating step and an end date, and it comes back to the reviewer on that date.

    GuardrailNo exception without an expiryEvidenceException register

  8. Rehearse the auditor’s question

    Ask the question the way an auditor would and answer it from the store alone, with nobody going to find a screenshot. Fix what breaks, then update the row.

    GuardrailDry run before the audit, and after a major changeEvidenceDry-run notes

The answer to the auditor comes out of the store in minutes: both exports, the decisions, the tickets, and the reviewer’s sign-off.

One control, several frameworks

Frameworks overlap heavily on IAM. Account review, least privilege, authentication and logging turn up in all of them. Writing a separate control for each framework produces duplicates that drift apart, so I write the control once and tag it.

  • Map many to one, and read the words. The review control above carries six tags. They are not word-for-word equal. Some cover all user accounts, some only privileged ones, some only service accounts. Where scope differs, the control statement widens until it covers the largest scope, or a second row covers the remainder.
  • Treat the mapping as my reading. A framework does not say a particular control meets it. The auditor and the framework owner decide that. The tag records who mapped it, when, and on which version of the standard.
  • Keep the register framework-neutral. Frameworks change versions. A register organized by control survives a new standard. One organized by a standard does not.

Evidence the audited cannot edit

If the people running a control also control its evidence, the evidence is only as good as their honesty on the worst day. The people involved can be entirely trustworthy and the arrangement is still weak, because the auditor cannot tell the difference.

NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5 covers this under AU-9, protection of audit information. The base control is to protect audit information and audit logging tools from unauthorized access, modification and deletion, and to alert when it happens. Its enhancements describe the means. They include storing audit records on a physically different system from the one audited (AU-9(2)), limiting who can manage audit logging (AU-9(4)), dual authorization for moving or deleting audit information (AU-9(5)) and read-only access (AU-9(6)). PCI DSS v4.0 has the same intent in requirement 10.3.2, which asks that audit log files are protected to prevent modifications by individuals.

  • Separate the store from the system. Evidence for a platform lives somewhere the platform’s admins cannot write.
  • Write once, read many. Control owners and admins read. A small, named group holds the right to manage the store itself, and its changes are logged.
  • Prove the lock. Try to edit an item with an owner account and an admin account, and keep the failed attempt as evidence.

Gaps and exceptions, with an end date

Some controls will not work as written, and some evidence will not exist. A register that shows everything green is either wrong or hiding things. A good one shows a few honest gaps, each with a plan.

  • A gap is a control or its evidence falling short of what the register says it should be. It is recorded the day it is found.
  • An exception is a decision to accept the shortfall for a while. It needs an owner who accepts the risk, a reason, a compensating step, and an expiry date.
  • Expiry forces a decision. On the date, the exception is closed because it is fixed, renewed by the same approver with a fresh reason, or escalated. Letting it run on silently is the one thing it cannot do.

Preparing for internal and external audit

Internal and external audits ask for similar things in different tones. Internal audit usually wants to find problems early and will accept a recorded gap with a plan. External audit wants the control demonstrated across the period and has less room for surprises. The same register and store serve both.

  • Walk the scope with the auditor first. Which controls, which period, which systems. Agree it in writing so the register and the request list line up.
  • Run the register against the request list. Every item the auditor asks for should map to a row. Items that do not map point to a missing control or a missing row.
  • Hand over by link and permission, not by email. Give the auditor read access to the relevant part of the store. Everything they pull is logged, and there is one copy of each item.
  • Treat findings as register changes. A finding becomes an update to the control, the configuration or the evidence plan, with an owner and a date, and goes back through the chain.

Governance: who owns what

RoleOwns
Control ownerThat the control is designed well and works. Answers for gaps and signs exceptions for their area.
Evidence ownerThat the evidence is collected on time, complete and saved with its source and query. Keeps the collection job running.
ReviewerAn independent check that the evidence is complete, current and answers the objective. Not the control owner and not the collector.
Register keeperThe structure of the register, framework tags and the review rhythm. Does not own the controls themselves.
Internal auditIndependent testing of the whole arrangement, separate from those who run the controls and those who review them.
  • Separate the three jobs. The person who runs a control, the person who collects its evidence and the person who reviews it should not all be the same person. Where a small team makes that impossible, record the overlap as an exception with a compensating check.
  • Review on a rhythm. The register gets a regular review, and an extra one when a system, an owner or a framework version changes.

What tends to go wrong

  • The register becomes a spreadsheet nobody opens. If updating it is separate work from changing a system, it will fall behind. Tie the register change to the system change.
  • Evidence is built for the audit. A burst of screenshots the week before only proves the week before. Collection by job fixes this.
  • Everything gets mapped to everything. Tagging a control to a requirement it only loosely touches makes the mapping look complete and wastes the auditor’s time. Tag only what the control really answers.
  • Review turns into signing. If the reviewer sees only a form and never the list, the review proves nothing. The record must hold the list as it stood and the decision for each item.
  • Exceptions pile up. Without expiry dates the register fills with accepted risk nobody remembers accepting.

What I have learned to do differently

These are habits I keep when I work on controls and evidence. They are not a checklist from any one engagement.

  • Start from the question, then work backwards. I write down what an auditor would ask and trace it back to the system that could answer it. That finds missing evidence faster than reading the standard top to bottom.
  • Name one owner, even when it is awkward. I would rather have an owner who objects early than a shared owner who is surprised later.
  • Test the evidence, not only the control. I try to answer a question from the store with nobody helping. If it takes a conversation, the evidence is not ready.
  • Say what the control does not cover. Writing the limits down in the register protects the owner and keeps the auditor’s scope honest.
  • Treat a gap as normal news. If people are afraid to report one, they will hide it. I ask for gaps early and reward the people who raise them.
