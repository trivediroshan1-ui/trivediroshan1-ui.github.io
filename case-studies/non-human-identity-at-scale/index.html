Non-Human Identity at Scale

How to find the service accounts, keys, tokens and agents nobody wrote down, give each one an owner, rank them by risk, and replace the long-lived ones with credentials that expire.

Credential Management Platform Owner & PAM Product Owner · Last reviewed 1 Oct 2026

The short version

  • Machine identities get created faster than anyone records them. The first job is one inventory, with a named owner against every entry and a flag on every entry that has none.
  • A simple score turns that inventory into a queue. I rank on three things: how long since the secret changed, how much it can do, and whether anyone owns it. Evidence of exposure and last use come next.
  • The best fix removes the secret. Where a workload can use a role, federation or a short-lived token, do that before you rotate a static key.

The problem

A non-human identity is anything that signs in without a person at the keyboard: a service account, an API key, a token, a certificate, a workload identity, an OAuth app, a bot, and more and more often an AI agent. Each one is a way into a system, and each one raises the same questions as a person’s account. Who is it for? What can it reach? When was it last changed? Who would notice if it were misused?

The difference is how they come into being. A developer creates a service account to unblock a release. A pipeline needs a token, so someone makes one. A vendor integration asks for an OAuth grant, and an admin clicks yes. None of these goes through the process a new employee goes through, and none of them has a manager who changes jobs and hands the account over.

So ownership gets lost at handoff, credentials outlive the systems that issued them, and audits fall back on belief. “We think the team rotates these” is a sentence I have heard in more than one review, and it is not evidence. OWASP’s Non-Human Identities Top 10 puts improper offboarding first, which it describes as inadequate deactivation or removal of non-human identities when they are no longer needed. That is the same failure in different words.

I have left out any ratio of machines to people. Figures like that circulate widely, and I could not trace one to a primary source with a method I trust. The program does not depend on the number. It depends on knowing your own inventory.

How the credentials themselves are stored, issued and rotated is covered in Credential Management Platform Strategy. This page picks up one step earlier: finding what exists, deciding who answers for it, and deciding what to fix first.

How it fits together

Five kinds of source tell you what exists. Cloud IAM lists roles, access keys and app registrations. CI/CD systems list pipeline variables and tokens. Code scanning finds secrets sitting in repositories and their history. Vaults know what is stored, when it last rotated and when it was last read. Directories hold the service accounts. Each source sees a different slice, and the same identity often appears in two or three of them under different names.

Everything flows into one inventory. From there each record needs an owner, then a score, and then it lands in a queue where someone fixes it. A re-scan afterwards confirms the fix, and an evidence log keeps who, what and when for every step. The scanning is read-only. Fixing happens in the source system, by the owner.

The workflow, step by step

Seven steps. Each has a guardrail, which is the rule that keeps the step honest, and the evidence an auditor can ask to see.

  1. Discover from every source you can reach

    Start with the two or three sources that hold the most: usually cloud IAM, the main vault and the code repositories. Keep a register of sources, with a person who answers for each one, so a gap in coverage is visible and not silent.

    GuardrailRead-only discovery access, every source has an ownerEvidenceSource register with last successful scan date

  2. Merge into one inventory

    Give each identity one record with a few required fields: type, system, environment, created date, last rotated, last used, privilege level, owner and the source that found it. When two sources describe the same identity, merge them. When a record is missing a field, flag it and keep it. A half-filled record is still better than no record.

    GuardrailRequired fields, and incomplete records are flagged, not droppedEvidenceInventory export with field completeness

  3. Assign a named owner

    An owner is a person or a team that will answer for the identity, change it and retire it. Where nobody claims it, the record goes to the owner of the system it touches, and from there to a review queue with a deadline. Ownership has to be accepted, because being named by someone else does not make a person accountable.

    GuardrailNo owner within the agreed time raises the score and routes to reviewEvidenceOwner acceptance record

  4. Score every identity the same way

    Use one formula, version it, and store the inputs next to the result so anyone can see why an identity ranks where it does. The factors and a worked example are in the next section. The score orders the work. It says nothing about the quality of a team.

    GuardrailOne versioned formula, inputs kept with the scoreEvidenceScore history per identity

  5. Fix at the source, in a fixed order

    Work down the queue. For each identity ask four questions in order. Is it still used? If not, retire it. Can the workload use a role, federation or a short-lived token? If so, replace the secret. Is the access wider than the job needs? Cut it. Is a static secret still unavoidable? Rotate it, and set the next rotation. Retiring comes before rotating, because rotating something nobody uses only adds work.

    GuardrailRetire first, remove the secret second, rotate lastEvidenceChange record in the system that owns the identity

  6. Verify, then close

    A fix is closed when a re-scan shows the change and the owner confirms it. If the owner stays silent, the item escalates. Closing a ticket on someone’s word is how stale findings come back at the next audit.

    GuardrailClosure needs a re-scan result, and a ticket status alone is not enoughEvidenceBefore and after scan, owner confirmation

  7. Guard the front door

    The cleanup only holds if new identities arrive through a path that asks for an owner, a purpose and an expiry. Secret scanning on commits keeps new keys out of code. Anything created outside the path shows up in the next scan and counts against the owner of the system.

    GuardrailCreation path requires owner and expiry, secret scanning on commitEvidenceBlocked-commit log, exceptions register

Scoring: how I rank the list

A flat list of thousands of identities cannot be worked. A short score does the sorting. The model I use on this site has three factors. I keep it small on purpose, because a score people can recompute in their head gets trusted, and one they cannot gets ignored.

FactorHow it is calculatedWhy it is there
StalenessDays since the credential last changed, divided by 30, capped at 10, then multiplied by 0.55A secret that has not changed has had more time to leak and be copied.
PrivilegeStandard counts 1, Elevated 2 and Admin 3.2, then multiplied by 1.6It sets the blast radius. The same leak costs far more at Admin.
Ownership gapAdd 3.5 if no owner is recordedWith no owner there is nobody to rotate, scope down or retire it.

Add the three results, round to a whole number and keep it between 1 and 10. A score of 7 or more counts as high risk, 4 to 6 as medium and below that as low. These weights are starting values for the demonstration, and they are no standard. In a real program I would tune them with the data owner and the people who answer for incidents, then freeze the version.

Two policies change the result and are easy to explain. Enforcing a 90-day rotation caps the staleness input at 90 days. Requiring an owner within 30 days of creation removes the ownership gap.

The lesson I draw from this is that ownership and rotation cut the score a long way and still left it at 7. The privilege cut and the removal of the stored secret finished the job. That is why the order in step 5 starts with retiring and replacing, and leaves rotation for last.

What the score leaves out matters too. It does not see whether the secret has already been found in code, or whether the identity has been used in the last quarter. Once the inventory is trustworthy I add those two as extra factors. An identity with a known exposure moves to the top whatever its other numbers say.

Removing the secret: federation and short-lived tokens

Rotation limits how long a stolen secret works. Removing the stored secret removes the thing to steal. Most major platforms now document a way for a workload to prove who it is and receive a credential that expires on its own.

Roles on the platform itself. AWS’s IAM guidance says workloads on its compute services should use temporary credentials delivered with an IAM role, and that there is no need to distribute long-lived credentials for an IAM user to workloads running on AWS. The same guidance says that where long-term keys cannot be avoided, you should update them when needed, and use last accessed information to find and remove credentials you no longer need.

Federation for pipelines and outside workloads. GitHub Actions can request a signed OIDC token for each run and exchange it with a cloud provider for a short-lived access token, so no cloud credential has to be stored as a long-lived GitHub secret. The workflow needs the id-token: write permission, which does not by itself let it write to any resource. GitHub also says you must define at least one condition on the token’s claims, so that untrusted repositories cannot request tokens for your cloud resources. The subject claim can narrow that to an environment, a branch or pull requests. Google Cloud describes Workload Identity Federation as a way to authenticate workloads outside Google Cloud without a static service account key. Microsoft Entra documents the same pattern for software outside Azure, including GitHub Actions and Kubernetes workloads. OWASP’s list includes insecure cloud deployment configurations and mentions improperly validated OIDC tokens in that item, so the trust condition is where review effort belongs after the move.

Workload identity across platforms. SPIFFE defines an ID for a workload in the form spiffe://trust-domain/workload and a verifiable document for it, called an SVID, as either an X.509 certificate or a JWT. The project notes that X.509 is preferred, because tokens are susceptible to replay. Through the Workload API a workload gets its credentials without holding an authentication secret, and the keys are short-lived and rotated automatically. SPIRE is the reference implementation: a server issues identities for a trust domain, an agent runs on each node, the agent is attested when it first connects, and each workload is attested by matching its properties to a registration entry. I reach for this when many services across several clusters or clouds need to identify each other. Where everything sits in one cloud, that cloud’s native roles are simpler to run.

NSA and CISA’s 2024 cloud identity guidance points the same way. It asks for keys generated for short-term use, least privilege, and secrets kept in a secrets management tool and never in plain text or in source code.

How to roll it out

  1. 1. Agree scope and who decidesWrite down which kinds of identity are in the first release and who signs off the formula, the owner rules and the exceptions.Done whenScope, decision-maker and escalation path are written and agreed.
  2. 2. Build a first inventory from two sourcesPick cloud IAM and the main vault, or whichever sources hold the most. Merge them and show the result to the system owners.Done whenThere is one list with required fields, and the gaps are visible.
  3. 3. Run an ownership sweepTake the identities with no owner, group them by system, and walk each group through with the people who run that system.Done whenEach identity has an accepted owner or a dated review item.
  4. 4. Score and work the top of the listTurn on the formula and agree a working limit for the queue. Fix the highest scores first, in the order from step 5 of the workflow.Done whenThe top slice is closed with re-scan evidence.
  5. 5. Move pipelines and cloud workloads off stored keysStart where a role or federation is available and the blast radius is large. Leave the awkward legacy cases on a named exception list.Done whenNew pipelines authenticate by federation by default.
  6. 6. Guard creation and run it as a routineRequire owner and expiry at creation, schedule rescans and put owner attestation on a calendar.Done whenNew identities outside the path appear in the next scan and are acted on.

A reader on LinkedIn, Gelson Monteiro, made the point that Zero Standing Privilege only holds once ownership is enforced, and that finding non-human identities is getting easier while finding a person willing to be accountable for one is harder. I agree. Shrinking an access window without a lifecycle underneath it just times out access that nobody remembers approving.
  • Measure what moves. Share of identities with an accepted owner, count of high-scoring identities, age of the oldest unrotated secret, share using short-lived credentials, and time from discovery to closure.

What tends to go wrong

  • Nobody wants the owner field. Being named owner feels like taking on risk. If the only consequence is a nagging email, people decline or click through. Make the route to ownership easy and the alternative clear, and expect the first sweep to be slow.
  • Sources disagree. The same identity has a different name in the directory, the cloud and the vault. Merging them takes judgement, and a wrong merge hides a real finding.
  • A big queue gets ignored. Thousands of tickets on day one teach everyone to stop looking. Release a working slice at a time.
  • Federation moves the risk. A loose trust condition can be worse than the key it replaced, since it is easy to miss. Review the rules as carefully as you reviewed the secrets.
  • The score gets treated as truth. It orders the work and nothing more. A low score on an identity that is plainly exposed still gets fixed.
  • Agents multiply faster than reviews. AI agents hold keys, tokens and delegated access like any other machine identity, and they appear faster than a quarterly review can follow. Treat them as part of this inventory from the start. See The AI Stack Nobody Approved.

What I have learned to do differently

  • I start with ownership and treat scoring as the second job. A perfect score on an ownerless list changes nothing. When I begin with the people who run the systems, the rest goes faster.
  • I show the inventory to owners before I publish it. They find the mistakes in minutes, and they are more willing to accept an entry they have seen than one that appears in a report.
  • I keep the formula small and write the arithmetic down. If a system owner can recompute their own score on paper, the argument moves from the number to the fix.
  • I ask whether the secret is needed before I ask how to rotate it. The best answer is often to retire or replace, and asking first saves a lot of work.
  • I treat the trust rules for federation as inventory too. They are small and easy to forget, and they decide who gets in.
  • I close items on evidence. A re-scan result goes in the ticket. I would rather close fewer items and defend each one.
  • I put expiry in at creation. Cleaning up later is always more expensive than asking for an end date on day one.
