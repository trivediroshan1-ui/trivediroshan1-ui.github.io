PAM Modernization & Zero Standing Privilege

How to move privileged access from permanent admin rights to short, approved, recorded sessions, and how to prove to an auditor that it works.

Product and architecture lead · Last reviewed 30 Sep 2026

The short version

  • Most PAM programs stop once the passwords are in a vault. The risk that is left is access that never expires.
  • Modernizing means four things working together: vault the secret, rotate it, put a recorded session in front of it, and grant access only for the length of the task.
  • Every one of those steps has to leave evidence an auditor can check without asking you to explain it.

The problem

Privileged accounts are the ones that can change the system itself: domain admins, database owners, root on servers, cloud administrators, and service accounts with wide rights. Attackers want them because a valid account looks like normal activity. MITRE ATT&CK tracks this as Valid Accounts (T1078).

Standing privilege makes it worse. If an admin group has forty members, then forty people, and any malware running as them, can reach every system that group covers, at any hour, whether or not they are doing admin work. When an admin signs in interactively to a workstation, credential material can stay in that machine’s memory. That is why credential dumping (T1003) is such a common step right after the first foothold.

Where PAM programs stall

  • The vault is there, but the old door is still open. Passwords are stored centrally, yet admins can still log in directly because nobody closed the direct path.
  • Rotation skips the hard accounts. The accounts nobody dares touch are the ones hard-coded in scripts or shared between teams.
  • Session recording is patchy. Some systems are covered and some are not, so a reviewer cannot answer “what did they actually do?”
  • “Temporary” access is never taken back. Nobody owns the expiry.

The moving parts

A privileged session has to pass through a few controls, in order. If one is missing, the others can be walked around. The diagram shows the path a request takes, and where evidence is written along the way.

Products split this work differently, but the functions are the same. In CyberArk’s self-hosted architecture, for example, the Vault stores the secrets, the Central Policy Manager (CPM) generates new passwords and replaces them on the target machines, and the Privileged Session Manager (PSM) records sessions and can restrict commands. Other platforms combine or separate these roles. What matters is that all of the functions exist and that none can be skipped. CyberArk architecture reference.

A privileged request, start to finish

  1. Ask for a specific thing

    The engineer names the target, the reason, a ticket or change number, and how long they need. “Admin on everything” is not a valid request.

    GuardrailTicket must exist and be openEvidenceRequest record

  2. Prove who they are

    Sign-in goes through single sign-on with multi-factor authentication. For the most sensitive systems, that means phishing-resistant methods such as FIDO2 keys, not SMS or push approvals.

    GuardrailNo MFA, no requestEvidenceSign-in log

  3. Approve by policy, or by a person

    Low-risk, pre-agreed roles can be approved automatically. Access to production or to the identity systems themselves needs a named human, and for the most critical, two.

    GuardrailRequester cannot approve their own requestEvidenceApprover, time, reason

  4. Connect through the broker

    The engineer opens a session through the PAM broker. The broker uses the credential on their behalf, so the person never sees or types the password.

    GuardrailDirect login to the target is blocked at the networkEvidenceFull session recording

  5. Work, and be recorded

    Commands and screens are recorded. Where the platform supports it, dangerous commands can be blocked or flagged in real time.

    GuardrailRecording cannot be turned off by the userEvidenceSearchable recording tied to the ticket

  6. Access ends on its own

    When the time runs out or the session closes, access is removed and the secret is changed, so a copied password is worthless.

    GuardrailExpiry is enforced by the system, not by memoryEvidenceRotation log

Example: a database fix at 2 a.m.

This is an illustration, not a specific event.

Before

  • One shared database admin password, known to a whole team.
  • Not changed for over a year, because changing it might break a script.
  • The engineer logs in directly. Nothing records what they ran.
  • After an incident, nobody can say who did what.

After

  • The engineer asks for two hours on that database, citing the incident ticket.
  • The on-call manager approves from their phone.
  • The session runs through the broker and is recorded.
  • At the end, access closes and the credential is rotated. The recording is linked to the ticket.

What “zero standing” changes

Zero Standing Privilege means nobody holds admin rights by default. Access is created for a task and removed when it ends. This lines up with a tenet in NIST’s Zero Trust Architecture (SP 800-207): access to each resource is granted per session, with the least privilege needed to finish the task.

How to get there without stalling

The order matters. Each phase should be finished, and proven, before the next one starts.

  1. 1. Find every privileged accountHuman admins, service accounts, cloud roles, SSH keys and shared logins. You cannot protect an account you have not found.Done whenEvery privileged account has a named owner.
  2. 2. Vault and rotate the most critical firstStart with the identity systems, domain admins and vault administrators, then work outward.Done whenCritical accounts rotate automatically and no one knows the current password.
  3. 3. Put sessions behind a brokerRoute admin access through a proxy that records it.Done whenDirect admin login to those targets is blocked, not just discouraged.
  4. 4. Remove standing access for peopleReplace permanent admin group membership with approved, time-boxed access. Keep one tested break-glass path.Done whenStanding membership of privileged groups is close to zero, with every exception owned.
  5. 5. Extend to machinesApplications and pipelines that use secrets move to a secrets manager or short-lived credentials, so nothing important is hard-coded.Done whenScans of code and configuration find no live credentials.

Audit: the questions, and the evidence

Design for the questions an auditor will ask, and make sure the answer comes out of a system, not out of someone’s memory.

QuestionWhere the answer comes from
Who had privileged access to this system on this date?Approval records plus broker session logs
Can you show who used a shared or generic account?Check-out log tied to a named person and a recording
How often are privileged passwords changed?Rotation history from the vault
Who reviews privileged access, and how often?Signed review records with removals listed
What happens in an emergency?Break-glass procedure, last test date, alert history

Internal auditIndependent checking. Kept separate from the people who run the controls.
  • Exceptions expire. Every exception has an owner, a reason, a compensating control and an end date. It is renewed on purpose or it goes away.
  • Measure the trend, not a vanity number. Useful measures: share of privileged accounts vaulted, share that rotate, standing members of privileged groups, time to grant access, and break-glass uses reviewed within a day.
  • Review on a rhythm. Privileged access reviewed every quarter, and the policy every year.

What tends to go wrong

  • Friction breeds workarounds. If getting access takes an hour, people will keep a back-door account. Make the approved path the quickest one.
  • The PAM platform becomes the target. It holds the keys to everything, so it needs high availability, its own strict access, and a tested way in when it is down.
  • Legacy applications. Some cannot use a rotated password or a proxy. Track them as named exceptions instead of leaving them out of scope quietly.
  • Trying to do everything at once. Programs that start with every account and every platform tend to stall. Finish the critical tier first.
