The MCP approval lab, in depth

What I did, why I did it, what I followed, what three rounds of testing found, and what a firm can take from it. Written plainly, with the limits left in.

The short version

AI agents now call real tools through MCP, the Model Context Protocol. Some of those calls matter: a deploy, a role change, a payment. The usual answer is “a person approves it”. I wanted to find out whether that approval actually holds. Does it cover exactly what was asked, work once, expire, and leave a record you can check later?

So I built a small open-source approval gateway, called HumanGate, and then spent three rounds trying to break it. About 210 scripted checks in total. Most things held. Several did not, and I fixed what I could and wrote down what I could not. Everything uses made-up tools and made-up data.

Why I did this

  • Approval is the control everyone points to. “Human in the loop” is said a lot and tested rarely. I wanted to see what happens at the edges: a changed argument, a second click, a lost reply, an expired approval.
  • My work is access and identity. In privileged access, a yes from a person has always had to be tied to one exact action, once, with a record. Agents bring the same question back in a new place.
  • I prefer evidence to opinion. A write-up of what a gate does when you push on it is more useful than a list of things it should do.
  • It is a personal project. It runs on my own machines with nothing real in it. It says nothing about any employer or any product I work with.

What I built

The gateway sits between an agent and its tools. Fixed rules score each call. Low-risk calls run. Risky ones wait for a person with the right role. The approval is tied to a hash of the exact request, works once, and expires. Every decision, including every refusal, goes into a hash-chained audit log. An AI analyst can write advice for the reviewer, but it cannot approve, deny or change the score.

The code is public, with tests. You can also try it in your browser, with a scenario runner and a queue of requests, no setup.

What I did, in three rounds

Round one: 76 cases

I wrote down what I expected before each run, then ran each case once. A real MCP client (the official Inspector) clicked by hand, a script against the real endpoint, and a browser lab where I could make a tool lose its reply. 72 matched my guess, 1 was different, 3 were information only. Six of my guesses were wrong, which taught me the most.

I also ran real Claude Code as the MCP client, on one laptop, for one path: it connected, asked for a production deploy, waited, ran it after approval, and was refused on a second use. I then recomputed the audit log hashes outside the gateway (190 events, no break). In the browser lab I checked how hidden characters, long text, markup and a 44-request queue look to a reviewer.

Round three: 46 more scripted checks, on Linux and the Mac

A reader of my last post asked what is unclear about a permit and receipt layer, what would make someone try it and what is missing. In my lab the permit is the approval and the receipt is the answer that comes back after the run, so I looked at both properly. The agent gets proof that something ran, but not who approved it or under which policy. The tool at the end gets no proof of the approval at all.

I also checked how the gateway treats tokens, MFA and SSO claims, robots and other non-human identities, secrets and passwords, and I ran 20 made-up tools for government, schools and colleges, a company’s ERP, IT and identity teams, robots and a plant. They behaved as the rules say. Three of them, written carelessly on purpose, landed one tier lower, which shows that the tool list needs an owner. The Mac gave the same results as Linux. Nothing in the gateway code changed. Eight more limits are written down. The PDF has the topic-by-topic map, from IAM to governance.

What I followed

  • Expected first, then run. Every case has a line saying what I expected. A wrong guess is kept, not tidied away.
  • One run each. No cherry-picking a good run. Scripts were also re-run on a Mac and a Linux box, with checksums so the same script is the same script.
  • Synthetic only. Made-up tools, made-up people, temporary databases, nothing that touches a real system.
  • The MCP specification. The 2025-11-25 version with its handshake, and the 2026-07-28 version without one. The gateway tracks both, and I tested a client of each kind.
  • The OWASP MCP Top 10. My own reading of where each item shows up in the lab. Not an OWASP assessment and not a claim of coverage.
  • A written threat model. In the repository, updated this round with the limits below.
  • Prior art. Before saying anything is new, I looked. Tying an approval to an exact request, and letting harmless fields vary, are already discussed by several public projects. I do not claim either.

Two lessons from building the harmless-fields prototype. A key named __proto__ silently disappeared from the hash with plain assignment. And the execute path ignored one kind of policy refusal until I made it respect any denial. Anyone building this has to check the allowed-to-vary fields everywhere the hash is recomputed.

Across 17 hostile payloads aimed at those fields, none executed on the prototype. Against today’s gateway, 7 of 10 similar payloads went through in a field that is outside the hash. That is why the limits matter.

How this can help a firm

This is not a product pitch or a certification. It is a way of asking better questions before an agent gets a production tool.

  • Ask what exactly the approval covers. The arguments, the target, the tool’s own definition, the policy version? If the tool can change after approval and the yes still stands, that is the gap I found.
  • Ask whether it works once. And what a retry does. In my runs a repeat with the same key returned the stored result, a new key was blocked.
  • Look at what the reviewer sees. If the screen shows different words than the system stores, the approval is only as good as the screen.
  • Ask who the approver is. A typed name is not an identity. Real sign-in and roles matter more than any hash.
  • Keep a log you can check later. And save its head hash somewhere else, or a cut-off tail goes unnoticed.
  • Plan for a lost reply. Somebody has to settle what happened, and that record is only as honest as that person.
  • Remember the client has its own controls. Claude Code’s auto-mode stopped one of my calls before it reached the gateway. That layer is useful and separate. Do not rely on one layer.

The conformance runner is built so other gateways can be tried the same way. Only one adapter exists today, mine, so it is a starting point and not a benchmark.

What I still do not know

How this behaves with real clients at scale, with real people reviewing hundreds of requests, or with a real identity provider. Whether the allowed-to-vary idea survives a determined attacker I did not think of. Whether my 17 hostile payloads were the right 17. A search for prior art is not proof of absence.

Honest limits

One run each, on my machines, with synthetic tools. This shows what this gateway did on these cases. It does not prove it secure, and it is not a review of any product or company.

Reproduce it

Clone the repository, run npm test, then the scripts in scripts/. Each prints what it expected and what it got. The repository needs Node 24; I ran on Node 22 and saw the warning.
