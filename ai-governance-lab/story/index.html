AI governance lab

Why I built this lab

I spent years mapping access controls for auditors. When AI agents arrived with the same promises, I wanted to see whether the controls really hold, so I tested them.

My story, in my words

I used to do control mapping for PAM tools against IAM policies, created internally and verified by external auditors against standards like NIST, PCI, HIPAA and ISO 27001, depending on the project: finance, healthcare or an information security firm.

One question was always at the back of my mind.

Are we serious about these controls we map from the vendor, and do they really adhere to the organisation’s policy? It still fascinates me.

Then the AI era started. I kept hearing that AI hooks will help, that agents will map policies, that AI identification will automate identity, and that a Centre of Excellence for AI governance will sit at the top and decide what level of permission each agent should get. Later I heard that AI agents will use PAM or identity APIs to stay within governance and meet compliance.

But I never heard what the use cases are, what policies we are trying to manage, how the governance factor is aligned, or who the certifier is. And what if an AI agent goes vague and makes its own decisions?

That is when my mind started building towards this AI governance test on my private laptop. Before that, I read white papers from NIST, ISO and the IGA and PAM vendors on how they manage, discover and automate the identity life cycle, accounts linked to an identity, non-human identities and agents, so that I had the knowledge before I built anything.

From mapping sheets to a test bench

  1. Mapping controls

    Vendor controls matched to policy, then to audit standards, for finance, healthcare and security firms.

  2. The AI promise

    Agents will map policies, AI will discover and automate identities, a central team will set each agent’s access.

  3. The gap

    No clear use cases, no named policy per agent, no certifier, no answer for an agent that decides on its own.

  4. The lab

    A private test on made-up data. Expected results written first, then 59 cases run.

The questions nobody answered

  • Which use cases are we talking about?
  • Which policy is each agent bound to?
  • How does the governance part line up with an actual control?
  • Who is the certifier for an agent’s decision?
  • If an agent goes off script and decides on its own, what stops it, and what proves it?

Why “read access” is not a small thing

These are everyday situations. None of them needs an attacker.

Bank

The support bot

A bot with “read access” can see PAN, Aadhaar and transactions. If it repeats them in a chat, or sends them to an outside model, nobody was hacked. It did what it was allowed to do.

Corporate

The helpful assistant

An assistant that reads mail and shared drives can surface salary or legal files to someone who was never meant to see them.

Tools and apps

The one-time Allow

A plugin is approved once and then reads the whole workspace, every day, with the same rights as the person who clicked.

Bots and agents

The shared token

A bot on a shared or long-lived token looks exactly like a real employee in the log. After a mistake, nobody can tell who did what.

So I built the test

The company is made up: Kaveri Finance, a small lender. The Aadhaar, PAN and card numbers are invented, but they pass the real checksums, so the tests behave like real data. I wrote the expected result of every case and saved it in git before running anything.

4 of 10unseen items caught by all three filters
9 of 9requests where a real assistant followed one written policy
What heldAccess register, approvals, expiry, kill switches, short-lived agent tokens and the tamper-evident log.
What brokeA plain text filter. Hinglish, numbers in Hindi words, split messages and quoted attacks got through.

This is a small test, on one laptop, with made-up data. The 100% filter score on 200 samples is not a result I trust, because I tuned the filter on those samples.

What I take back to my old audit questions

A control that looks right in a mapping sheet still has to be tested against the attack it is meant to stop. For AI agents, that means a named policy, its own short-lived identity, a human approval where it matters, and a log that shows who approved, not only that it ran.
