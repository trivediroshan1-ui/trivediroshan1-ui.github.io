Round 10 of the AI governance lab

A copied token, a leaver with a live session, a rehire, a contractor, an emergency account and an erasure request.

Last reviewed 14 November 2026

All rounds · Lab overview · Case explorer

Credentials, sessions and identity edge cases (round ten)

Rounds six to eight were about agents. Round ten goes back to the identity basics that were on my open list: what happens to a copied token, to a person who leaves while their session is still alive, to a disabled account when HR syncs, to a rehire, a surname change, an agency contractor, an emergency account, a request to erase a name from the log, and a clock that is wrong. These are the things an identity and access person gets asked about first.

The animation below follows one person through their time at the company and shows where each case sits. It is a design, tested here on small models I wrote, and it says who does each step.

  • HR, joiners and leavers
  • Identity provider and tokens
  • Apps and directory
  • An emergency
  • Logs and audit
  • Agents

  1. A person joins, or comes back HR, with the platform team

    A joiner, a rehire or a contractor starts. A rehire gets a fresh account and the new job's baseline, not the old account back. A contractor from an agency must have an end date, so the account expires on its own.

    • Hire event
    • Baseline access
    • End date

    AI’s part: None. This is plain process.

    Lab cases: C7C9

  2. Their details change HR sync to the directory

    A surname changes. The sync matches on the staff ID, which never changes, and sends only the fields that changed. It does not replace the whole record, so a disabled account stays disabled.

    • Staff ID
    • Changed fields only

    AI’s part: None.

    Lab cases: C6C8

  3. They sign in and get a token The identity provider

    The token is short-lived and tied to a key that the caller holds. A copied token alone is not enough. If the key is taken too, this does not help, and the lab says so.

    • Short token
    • Key proof

    AI’s part: None. An AI model is not involved in this round.

    Lab cases: C1C2

  4. An app checks the token on its own The application

    The app does not call the identity provider each time. It checks the signature, the expiry and a revoke list. It also checks its own clock against a trusted reference and refuses to decide if they differ too much.

    • Signature
    • Expiry
    • Clock check

    AI’s part: None.

    Lab cases: C12

  5. The person leaves and a signal goes out The identity provider, signed

    HR ends the person's employment. The identity provider sends a signed revoke signal. The app ignores unsigned messages, and orders signals by the time on the event, so a late message cannot undo a newer one.

    • Signed signal
    • Event time

    AI’s part: None.

    Lab cases: C3C4C5

  6. An emergency happens The on-call engineer

    A break-glass account is used at 2 am. A person is alerted within a minute, and when the account is checked back in its password is changed.

    • Alert
    • New password

    AI’s part: None.

    Lab cases: C10

  7. The log records it, without personal data The audit log

    The tamper-evident log holds a pseudonym, and the real name sits in a separate store. An erasure request deletes the name there, and the chain of hashes still checks.

    • Pseudonym
    • Hash chain

    AI’s part: None.

    Lab cases: C11

I wrote the expected result for 12 new cases (C1 to C12) and saved them before writing any code, on 25 Oct 2026 at 03:53 IST. All 12 came out as I guessed the first time. That says my guesses were easy, not that real systems are safe. I then switched off 12 controls one at a time in a copy of the code, and the matching case noticed 12 of 12. C2 has no such check, because it is a limit: nothing in the lab stops an attacker who has the token and the key.

What this does not show

  • Everything is a small model I wrote. There is no real identity provider, no real SCIM server, no real tenant and no AI model.
  • The proof of a key uses a shared secret as a stand-in. Real systems use a public key, as in DPoP or mutual TLS. Whether a real implementation checks the proof correctly is not tested.
  • C2 is a limit, not a fix. C11 does not test whether a pseudonym plus other fields could still identify a person.
  • C6 assumes a server that sets ‘active’ to true when a PUT leaves it out. Some servers do, some do not. Check yours.
  • I wrote the guesses and the code, and none of the 12 differed on the first run, so “as expected” shows the rules do what I intended, not that I thought of every case.
  • This round ran in a Linux sandbox with Python 3.11 and on my MacBook Air (Python 3.9.6) with the same verdicts: 12 of 12 as guessed and 12 of 12 control-off checks noticed. Both sets of files are on the run page.
